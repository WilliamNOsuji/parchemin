The storm glass

The glass had been clear all morning, which was how Mara knew her father was lying. He kept it on the sill of the lamp room where the light could get at it, a sealed bulb of water and crystals that was meant to cloud before weather, and it sat there as clear as a held breath while the sea went the colour of slate.

“It's a toy,” he said, when she asked. “Your grandmother's toy.” He did not look at it. He looked at the wick he was trimming, and the wick did not need trimming.

She had learned to read him the way she read the water: not by what moved, but by what refused to.

“Tobias saw a boat,” she said.

“Tobias sees a great many things.” The shears closed on nothing. “Go down and see to the bread.”

⁂

By four the glass had clouded, and he had not.

Library

The home screen keeps the web app's blocks and adds the one the research ranked first.

A sentence waitingNew. The last paragraph you wrote, plus the note you left about what happens next. Roadmap Tier 1 #1, the best-evidenced idea in the research and the only one not yet built.
Today, as a rhythmSeven bars replace “N days in a row”. Quiet days are shown as quiet. No streak is counted anywhere, which is what the README already promises.
Pick up where you left offThree stories, with the chapter you were in. One click reopens the chapter, not the story's front page.
Right sidebarSafekeeping at a glance and the drawer. Both exist in the web app; here they are always one glance away instead of a scroll down the home page.

Write

The chapter in the middle, everything else at the edges and collapsible to nothing.

Tabs in the title barAs Obsidian does on Windows. A tab can be a chapter, a character, the outline or safekeeping. The dot on a chapter tab is its status.
The library treeFolders, stories, chapters: the whole library is one tree, with status dots and word counts. Drag a story onto a folder or a chapter into a new order here.
The ribbonSix views and settings in a 44 px strip. Search is a view, not a box in the sidebar, so the sidebar stays a tree.
The manuscriptLiterata at 18 px on a 620 px measure, about 65 characters. The formatting bar shrinks to the pane header. The scene break stays a glyph.
The right sidebarThe web app's four panels become five tabs: Chapter plan, Dialogue, Snapshots, Notes, Unblock. Sections collapse like Obsidian's Outline and Backlinks.
The status barSays plainly where the words are and when they were last copied. Words today and the chapter's count sit on the right, with the two shortcuts a writer needs most.

Dialogue

The Dialogue panel built on Oct 10, in a sidebar wide enough for a script.

Who wants whatPer speaker and per chapter: the want, what they won't say and what they can't. The voice card is the hint beside the name.
The script draftOutside the manuscript. Tactics in brackets read back as beats. “Cover the names” hides the speakers so a stranger could still tell them apart.
The dialogue passThe writer's own ticks, with plain facts counted from the text beside each. Nothing scored, nothing suggested.
WidthThe sidebar grows from 300 to 440 px for this panel only, so the script keeps its line breaks.

Outline

The board, the finish line and the audit on one screen instead of three scrolls.

Rows, not cardsDrag handle, number, title and summary, status, the three plan marks (goal, conflict, turn), words. Reading across one row is the chapter check.
The finish lineProgress, the date, the daily figure from here, and “Pause the finish line”. The research warns about quotas, so the copy says it is a kindness and the pause is one click.
Read the whole outlineThe audit moves to the right sidebar, so the findings sit beside the rows they point at. Reading down is the book check.
Pace, honestlyThe last finding compares the needed pace with the last seven days and offers a later date with the number. It never says “behind”.

Cast

The cast grid from the roadmap, with the voice card and the hero check beside it.

One card per personName, role, voice with its family and tactic, what they want in a room, and their function: the thing nobody else in the book can do. A character with no function is a card you can see is empty.
Cast against each otherThe line under the grid reads the voices across the cast and names the pairs with built-in friction, and the pairs without.
The right sidebarVoice, flaw and need, and the hero check for the selected character. Unticked questions are listed first, because they are tomorrow's work.
Friction viewA second view (the quiet button) would draw the cast as a grid of who wants what against whom. Not drawn here; it needs a real cast to design against.

Focus mode

Ctrl+Shift+F. Everything but the current paragraph goes quiet.

What hidesRibbon, both sidebars, the pane header and the left half of the status bar. The title bar stays, because it is the window's.
What dimsEvery paragraph but the one with the caret, to 20%. Typewriter scrolling keeps the caret at the same height; it respects reduced motion.
The hintOne line, bottom right: how to leave, that typewriter scrolling is on, and today's words. Nothing else.

Command palette

Ctrl+P, as in Obsidian. Every action in the app, in the writer's own words.

Named like the buttons“A nudge, if you need one”, “Set this chapter aside”, “Cover the names”. The palette uses the same copy as the panels, so there is nothing new to learn.
Shortcuts shown, not requiredThe right column teaches the keys. Only the four most-used actions have one by default; writers can add more in settings.
Recent firstWith nothing typed, the list is the last ten commands used. Typing filters by any word in the name.

Canvas

Obsidian's canvas, with the story's own pieces as the cards.

Cards from the storyChapters, characters and settings are added from the story and stay live: a chapter card shows its current status, words and summary, and editing the summary here edits the outline. Notes, groups and sketches exist only on the board.
Named arrows“Won't ask”, “won't tell”, “saw it”, “keeps”. The arrows carry what the dialogue lesson calls the unsaid: who wants what from whom, and who knows what. The writer names every one. Nothing is drawn for them.
One surface for four ideasThe research's Tier 3 list has a who-knows-what grid, a threads grid, a promises ledger and a timeline. A board does all four without four features, and a board can be about anything.
GroupsA dashed region with a name. “What Mara doesn't know” is the whole chapter 8 plan in one box. Drag a card in and it moves with the group.
An open fileEach board is saved in the library folder as JSON Canvas, the open format Obsidian published for its own canvas. A Parchemin board opens in Obsidian, and the other way round.
Room to thinkThe library sidebar is collapsed here, because boards want width. The menu icon in the title bar brings it back. The toolbar at the bottom adds cards, and zoom sits where Obsidian keeps it.

Web

Obsidian's graph view, where the nodes are the writer's core ideas and the story's parts, and the threads are what the guide found in the text. Decided on Oct 10 (research.md, section 13).

Core ideas are documents“Silence as care”, “Inherited debt” and “The lamp” are three documents dropped into the Web, the way a note is dropped into Obsidian's graph. Each holds the writer's own words about what the story believes. The selected one is open in the sidebar.
Threads are evidenceA thread from a core idea to a character, symbol, setting, chapter or board means the guide found passages carrying it. Thickness is the count. Grey lines are plain co-occurrence from the text, no guide needed.
Deviation is thinChapter 6 has no thread to any core idea, so it sits at the edge with a dashed ring. “The lamp” has one thin thread, so the belief the story began with has gone quiet. The guide says so in words. No score anywhere.
Proposals are dashed“What is owed to the dead” is the guide's reading of chapters 2, 5 and 6. Accept it and it becomes yours. Refuse it and it is gone. Either way it never touches the manuscript.
Reading on saveOn by default once the guide is on, with “only when I ask” in the panel. Each read covers the chapter that changed, on the writer's own server. Without the guide, the Web still draws what is declared.
For you onlyThe Web and the core idea documents stay out of every export of the story. A rich story lets the reader build this web in their own head; this page is what they are trying to decipher.

Safekeeping

The biggest change the desktop app makes: the library is a folder.

Library folderObsidian's vault idea. One library.json holds everything, and each chapter is also a Markdown file beside it, so the book is readable without Parchemin. Roadmap Tier 2 #17 and decision 11.6 #5.
Between devices, for freePut the folder in the writer's own Dropbox, iCloud or OneDrive. That is roadmap group 3's “move my library” without building sync, and it stays true to “nothing leaves unless you carry it”.
No persistence promptThe web app has to ask the browser to keep data and explain Safari's seven-day rule. The desktop app does not, so that whole block disappears.
Export growsManuscript .docx and “Copy for AO3” from roadmap group 2 appear here as buttons, so the import/export work has a home.

What the desktop app changes

The web app stays as it is. The Electron build wraps the same code and adds what only a desktop can give.

Borrowed from Obsidian

  • The vault, renamed the library. A folder of plain files the writer can see, move and back up.
  • Ribbon, two sidebars, tabs, status bar. The same four regions, each collapsible, so the editor can take the whole window.
  • Command palette. Every action by name, with the shortcut taught beside it.
  • The canvas. Cards, groups and named arrows on a dotted board, saved as JSON Canvas. Here the cards are chapters, characters and settings.
  • Calm density. 13 px UI, 1 px borders, 6 px radii, one accent. Chrome is a shade darker than the page in both themes.
  • Follows the system theme. Obsidian defaults to dark. Parchemin follows the OS, with a toggle in the ribbon.

Left out on purpose

  • The graph of files and links. Parchemin's Web keeps the layout and changes the nodes: the writer's core ideas as documents, read by the guide against the text and the canvas so the story does not drift from its own beliefs. See the Web screen.
  • Plugin marketplace. No third-party code touches a manuscript. The licence lets anyone fork instead.
  • Markdown source mode. Markdown is the file format, never the view. Writers get a rich editor with smart quotes and scene breaks.
  • Sync as a subscription. The folder syncs through the writer's own account. Self-hosted sync can come later, as decided.
  • Properties and frontmatter. Chapter plans and status live in library.json; the Markdown files stay clean prose.

Only a desktop can do

  • Import by dropping files. Drop .docx, .md or .txt on the tree and get chapters. Roadmap group 2.
  • Native save dialogs. A backup always lands in a real folder, so “a backup counts once the file exists” is always true.
  • Keyboard first. Ctrl+P commands, Ctrl+Shift+F focus, Ctrl+Shift+S snapshot, Ctrl+D dialogue, Ctrl+N new chapter.
  • A quiet installer. One signed .exe, no account, no telemetry, updates off by default, and the “Your words” page on first run.
  • No browser to fight. No writing suggestions, no grammar extensions, no seven-day rule.

Open questions for the team

  • Chapters in the tree, or a chapter list inside the Write pane? The mockup puts them in the tree, as Obsidian would. The web app keeps a list beside the editor. Writers with 40 chapters and 6 stories will tell us.
  • What opens by default in the right sidebar? Chapter plan here. “Nothing” is the calmer answer, and the panel remembers its last state either way.
  • Markdown files: one per chapter, or one per story? One per chapter matches the tree and makes diffs readable in Dropbox's history.
  • Which shortcuts ship on, and which stay in the palette only? Four here. Obsidian ships almost none and lets people bind their own.
