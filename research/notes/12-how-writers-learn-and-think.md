## 0. How to read this file (method and honesty notes)

- Evidence labels: **well-supported** (multiple studies, a meta-analysis, or a replicated result), **plausible** (reasonable theory plus some data, or data from a different population), **popular but weak** (widely repeated, thin or contradicted evidence).
- Source tags used below:
  - `[F]` = I fetched the page or database record and read the abstract/text myself.
  - `[S]` = I saw it in a search-result summary only (the page itself was blocked or not fetched). Treat as "reported", not "confirmed".
  - `[M]` = from my own background knowledge, NOT verified in this session. Labelled "unverified". Do not turn into copy or claims without checking.
- Tool constraints during this run: many publisher and magazine domains returned 403 or "unable to fetch" (Paris Review, The Guardian, The New Yorker, Springer, SAGE, ScienceDirect, ResearchGate). ## 1. Executive summary

1. **Writing is cognitively overloaded by design.** Planning ideas, turning them into sentences, and reviewing text all draw on the same limited working-memory "central executive" (Kellogg's model, reviewed in a 17-year progress report). This is the best-supported idea in the field and is the main scientific reason to separate drafting from editing and to offer light, optional planning. `[F]` for the progress report; model details `[S]`.
2. **Planning helps on average, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient.** Outlining beat clustering for college writers (Kellogg 1987), and prewriting shows small to moderate effects in school meta-analyses (0.32 adolescents, 0.54 elementary). But undergraduate "planners", "revisers" and "mixed" writers all succeed, and a revision-heavy profile did as well or better in later work. Parchemin's "every craft tool is optional" principle is the evidence-consistent position. `[F]`/`[S]`.
3. **Experts differ from novices mainly in revising and in transforming ideas, not in typing faster.** Expert poets showed "associative playfulness" plus more deliberate revision; novices rarely revised (Peskin & Ellenbogen 2019, n=10 vs 10 think-aloud). Earlier work (Faigley, Flower, Atlas) found experts revise at macro level. Small samples, but consistent over 40 years. `[F]`.
4. **Practice schedule beats waiting for inspiration, but "write every day" is not proven.** Boice's small faculty experiment (scheduled brief sessions about 3.2 pages/day vs 0.8 vs 0.2 for waiting) is the origin of the claim; it has small N, a narrow population, and was criticised (Sword) and re-analysed. Habit formation takes a median of about 66 days with a range of 18-254 (Lally 2010, 96 people, a simple health habit). A gentle, self-chosen rhythm is defensible; a mandatory daily streak is not evidence-based. `[S]`/`[F]`.
5. **Writer's block is mostly not "no ideas".** A 2022 survey of 146 writers found blocks were mainly physiological and motivational and disrupted the composition stage more than idea generation. The strategies rated most useful were taking a break, switching projects, forcing yourself to keep going, and talking ideas over. This undercuts the popular belief that random prompts cure blocking. `[F]`.
6. **Prompts: little evidence for "random idea" prompts, more for "process" prompts.** I found no controlled study showing that creative-writing idea prompts improve finished work. I found a randomized study (n=73 children) where rhetorical-subgoal prompts raised text quality, and a preregistered experiment (n=332) where prompts tailored to task progress improved creative output. Good prompts ask about the state of your own story. `[F]` (ERIC abstracts).
7. **Handwriting vs typing is mostly a myth for adult drafting.** The famous "pen is mightier" note-taking result failed a direct replication (Urry 2021: 142 people, plus a meta-analysis of eight studies). Handwriting advantages exist for children's early literacy and word recall in small lab studies; the EEG paper that went viral in 2024 has been criticised. Evidence that typing speed harms text quality in older children was weak. Let writers use what they like. `[F]`.
8. **Incubation and low-demand time off help divergent thinking.** A meta-analysis of 117 studies found positive incubation effects, larger for divergent tasks and with undemanding activity during the break. Walking raised divergent-thinking scores in four experiments. These are lab tasks (unusual uses of objects), so applying them to story ideas is an extrapolation. `[F]`.
9. **Unfinished-plan notes help; "unfinished tasks stay in memory" does not hold.** Making a concrete plan for an unfulfilled goal removed intrusive thoughts and interference (5 experiments). A 2025 meta-analysis reportedly found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks (Zeigarnik) but a general tendency to resume them. This supports an end-of-session "what happens next" note, but not the usual folklore explanation of why Hemingway stopped mid-scene. `[F]` and `[S]` (via Wikipedia).
10. **Workshop critique is contested, with little controlled evidence either way.** Critics argue the workshop suits graduate students, breeds over-self-criticism, presumes a default reader, and silences the author. Alternatives (author-led discussion, "know-how", process-oriented courses) exist but are untested experimentally. Feedback in general is well-supported in school research (adult feedback 0.87, peer 0.58, self 0.62, computer 0.38), but that is not evidence for the Iowa format. `[F]`/`[S]`.

## 2. Findings by theme

### 2.1 Cognitive science of writing

**F1. Writing processes compete for working memory.** Rating: **well-supported** (for the general claim); **plausible** for specific component predictions.
- Evidence: A progress report on 17 years of dual-task research (Journal of Writing Research) concludes that planning, translating (turning ideas into sentences) and reviewing all depend on the central executive; translation also needs verbal working memory; the visuo-spatial sketchpad helps only when planning concrete, imagery-rich content; editing loads phonological working memory more than first theorised; visual and spatial working memory behave as separate systems. `[F]` https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/view/690 (the fetch did not return author names; I believe it is by Olive, unverified).
- Kellogg's model (1996) makes component-by-component predictions; one search summary says he suggested separating planning from translating relieves competition. `[S]` https://www.researchgate.net/publication/279589731_Working_Memory_in_Written_Composition_An_Evaluation_of_the_1996_Model (403 on fetch).
- Hayes 1996 reworked the earlier Flower & Hayes model: more weight on working memory, a visual-spatial dimension, integration of motivation and affect with cognition, and text interpretation (reading) as a core process. `[S]` https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271429714_A_new_framework_for_understanding_cognition_and_affect_in_writing . Hayes 2012 "Modeling and Remodeling Writing" exists (Written Communication) but I could not read it: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0741088312451260 `[S]` (403).
- Confidence: high on the overall claim; medium on details. Method is lab dual-task paradigms, mostly with students.
- Design implication: reduce simultaneous demands (formatting, structure decisions, correction) at the moment of drafting.

**F2. Knowledge-telling vs knowledge-transforming.** Rating: **plausible** (influential framework, mostly from school-age essay studies).
- Bereiter & Scardamalia (1987, *The Psychology of Written Composition*): novices "tell" what they know in the order it comes; experts treat writing as problem solving that transforms both the text and their own understanding. `[S]` https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333394475_The_Psychology_of_Written_Composition
- Caveat: derived from expository writing. For fiction, the analogue (finding out what the story is about by writing it) is anecdotally common but untested in controlled work.
- A JoWR paper on novice-to-expert development appears to describe a further stage ("knowledge-crafting") and says skill grows with sustained, feedback-rich practice, but the PDF was garbled when fetched, so treat as `[S]`-quality: https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/download/725/755/647

**F3. Planning/outlining: some evidence, many styles.** Rating: **plausible**; "you must outline" and "outlining kills creativity" are both **popular but weak**.
- Kellogg (1987), college students writing law-school-style tasks: "outlining, but not clustering, is an effective prewriting strategy"; judges rated content and style. `[F]` ERIC record via https://api.ies.ed.gov/eric/?search=Kellogg+Knowledge+and+Strategy+in+Writing&format=json&rows=3
- Kellogg (1988) JEP:LMC, "Attentional overload and writing performance: effects of rough draft and outline strategies" exists (DOI 10.1037/0278-7393.14.2.355, confirmed via Crossref). I could not read the abstract; the overload hypothesis is that pre-task planning frees working memory for generation. `[S]`/Crossref. Detailed result claims: unverified.
- Graham & Perin (2007) meta-analysis (grades 4-12; 123 documents, 154 effect sizes for writing quality): prewriting activities 0.32; strategy instruction 0.82; summarization 0.82; peer assistance 0.75; setting product goals 0.70; word processing 0.55; sentence combining 0.50; inquiry 0.32; process writing approach 0.32; study of models 0.25; grammar instruction -0.32. `[F]` ERIC: https://api.ies.ed.gov/eric/?search=Graham+Perin+Meta-Analysis+of+Writing+Instruction+for+Adolescent+Students&format=json&rows=3
- Elementary meta-analysis (Graham et al. 2012): pre-writing 0.54, strategy instruction about 1.02, SRSD 1.17, process approach 0.40. `[S]`
- Torrance, Thomas & Robinson: undergraduates cluster into planners, revisers and mixed writers; planners reported higher productivity; planning "neither a necessary nor a sufficient" condition for success. `[S]` (search summary of the 1994/2000 papers; a vdocument copy of the 1994 paper was listed).
- Revision-heavy profile outperformed three other profiles in a later undergraduate profile study (Journal of Writing Research-adjacent journal, 2020). `[S]` https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608020300030
- No controlled study of novelist outliners ("plotters") vs discovery writers ("pantsers") was found. This is an honest gap.
- Torrance, Fidalgo & Robledo (2015, Spanish sixth graders): process instruction changed students' processes but added no quality gain beyond product-goal-only instruction. `[F]` ERIC.

**F4. Why separate drafting from editing (and the evidence).** Rating: **plausible**.
- Theory: Elbow (1973) argued writers try to generate and judge simultaneously; separating them helps. Rose (1980, 1984): rigid rules and premature editing are linked with blocking; his 1983 data reportedly showed high-blockers paused longer, produced shorter drafts and spent more time planning than generating. `[F]` ERIC records of Rose's work (search "writer's block Rose cognitive").
- Mechanism evidence is the working-memory competition in F1. Direct experiments that compare "draft-then-edit" with "edit-as-you-go" in adult creative writers: I did not find any. 
- Counter-evidence: Faigley/Witte-style findings show experts revise a lot, and a revision-heavy profile can score well (F3). So "don't edit while drafting" is a tool for some writers, not a law.
- Design implication: offer an optional draft mode; do not enforce it.

**F5. Transcription skill and quality.** Rating: **plausible but weaker than assumed**.
- Process-disruption hypothesis: slow spelling or typing disrupts higher-level composing. In a study of sixth-graders' argumentative essays, spelling and typing fluency predicted composition fluency, but composition fluency barely predicted text quality; only slower typing within words predicted weaker thematic development. `[F]` https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9470714/
- Keystroke features predicted writing quality only weakly in a 2022 study (Conijn et al.). `[F]` ERIC record from the "keystroke logging" search. Useful for us because it also means process analytics would tell us little (and we will not collect them anyway).

**F6. Interruptions and context switching.** Rating: general effect **well-supported**; writing-specific effect **plausible, little direct evidence**.
- Leroy (2009) "attention residue": in two experiments, people who were interrupted on a task and moved to another did worse on a lexical task, in proportion to residue; people need to stop thinking about one task to perform well on the next. `[S]` https://www.researchgate.net/publication/46489122_Why_is_it_so_Hard_to_do_My_Work_The_Challenge_of_Attention_Residue_when_Switching_Between_Work_Tasks (work tasks, not writing).
- Masicampo & Baumeister (2011), five experiments: unfulfilled goals create intrusive thoughts and interference; forming a specific plan eliminated these effects. `[F]` PubMed 21688924.
- I found no experiment on interruptions during creative composition. Practitioners' lore (Hemingway: ruthless about not being interrupted) is anecdotal.

**F7. Zeigarnik effect and "stop mid-sentence".** Rating: the memory claim is **popular but weak**; the "resume tendency" is **plausible**.
- Several replications failed to find the original memory advantage, and a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis reportedly found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks but a general tendency to resume them (the Ovsiankina effect). `[S]` (via Wikipedia) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect
- Hemingway's own account is that he stopped when there was still something "in the deep part of the well". `[S]` https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway (Wikiquote, secondary).
- Implication: leaving a note about what comes next is supported (F6); claiming "stopping mid-scene keeps ideas alive" would overclaim.

### 2.2 Expertise

**F8. Expert vs novice writers.** Rating: **well-supported pattern, small samples**.
- Peskin & Ellenbogen (2019): 10 expert poets vs 10 novices thinking aloud while composing; experts showed associative playfulness and "surrendering of consciousness", more deliberate procedures and active revision, and attended to how textual elements evoke meaning; novices seldom revised and read literally. `[F]` ERIC record.
- ERIC records (1979-1989): skilled writers generated more ideas, organised earlier (Atlas 1979); experts made fewer but macro-level revisions (Faigley 1981); experts diagnosed global problems better (Flower et al. 1985); "many strategies employed by expert writers are teachable", especially planning (Hayes & Flower 1983). `[F]` ERIC (descriptions only; I did not read the papers).
- Deliberate practice: Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014) meta-analysis: deliberate practice explained 26% of variance in games, 21% music, 18% sports, 4% education, under 1% professions; "important, but not as important as has been argued". `[F]` PubMed 24986855. Writing was not one of the domains. A Wikipedia summary quoted about 40%, which conflicts with the primary abstract; I trust the abstract.
- Implication: "practice with feedback" is plausible and generally helpful, but we should not promise that practice alone makes a great writer.

### 2.3 Learning to write: practice, imitation, constraints, prompts

**F9. Habit and schedule.** Rating: scheduled brief sessions **plausible (small experiments)**; "write every day" **popular but weak**; "21 days to a habit" **debunked**.
- Boice (1983), college faculty who struggled to finish writing: five brief scheduled sessions a week produced 3.2 pages/day, writing only "when you feel like it" 0.8, waiting for inspiration 0.2; scheduled writers also reported more creative ideas. `[S]` https://www.research.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/RD/docs/Write-every-day-a-mantra-dismantled.pdf lists the discussion; the figures come from the search summary of the 1983 paper (Behaviour Research and Therapy 21). A follow-up on procrastination, busyness and bingeing: new faculty found brief daily sessions "effective and relatively unobtrusive". `[F]` PubMed 2610657.
- Limits: small groups, one population, self-reported creativity. Sword's "Write every day: a mantra dismantled" argues Boice's base is too narrow and the best pattern varies by person. `[F]` (summary of the PDF). A re-analysis "Optimal Levels of Writing Management: A Re-Analysis of Boice (1983)" exists (http://sdkrashen.com/content/articles/optimal_levels.pdf); the PDF was unreadable in my fetch, so its conclusions are unverified.
- Lally et al. (2010): 96 people, 84 days, median 66 days to plateau in automaticity, range 18-254, for simple health behaviours. `[S]` https://www.researchgate.net/publication/32898894_How_are_habits_formed_Modeling_habit_formation_in_the_real_world . I believe the paper also found that a single missed day did not derail habit formation, but I could not confirm this in the session: unverified `[M]`.
- Implication: a self-chosen rhythm and a streak that survives an unfinished day (already in PHILOSOPHY.md) is consistent with the evidence and with the philosophy. We must not claim "science says daily".

**F10. Goals and feedback.** Rating: **well-supported in school populations**.
- Setting product goals: ES 0.70 (Graham & Perin). Process goals with progress feedback gave fifth-graders the highest self-efficacy, strategy use and writing skill versus product goals or general goals (Schunk; Schunk & Swartz 1991). `[F]` ERIC records.
- Formative feedback on writing (grades 1-8 meta-analysis, Graham, Hebert & Harris 2015): feedback from adults 0.87, peers 0.58, the writer themself 0.62, computers 0.38. `[F]` ERIC.
- Adults writing fiction: no equivalent data. Implication: self-assessment tools (hero check, outline audit) have a plausible footing; computer feedback is the weakest of the four, so features should prompt reflection, not pose as a judge.

**F11. Reading and models.** Rating: reading helps writing **well-supported (school populations)**; "study of models" alone is **weak to moderate**.
- Reading-for-writing meta-analysis (Graham et al. 2018; 54 experiments with 5,018 students on reading instruction): teaching reading raised writing quality (ES 0.63); reading text or watching others read raised it (ES 0.44 writing quality). `[F]` ERIC.
- "Study of models" in Graham & Perin: 0.25 (modest).
- Mentor-text literature is mostly essays and practitioner articles; small empirical studies (Escobar Alméciga & Evans 2014; Batchelor & King 2014 on flash fiction) report benefits. `[F]` ERIC.
- "Reading like a writer" (Francine Prose) is popular craft advice; evidence for adults is anecdotal: **plausible**.

**F12. Imitation, copying, pastiche, sentence combining.** Rating: **plausible** (long craft tradition; little direct adult evidence).
- Franklin's method (verified in his *Autobiography*): note the "hints" of each sentence of a Spectator essay, set them aside a few days, then try to complete the paper from the hints, compare with the original, and correct faults; he also turned prose into verse and back. `[F]` https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20203/20203-h/20203-h.htm (short quote: "making short hints of the sentiment in each sentence"). This is an anecdote, not a study.
- Robert Louis Stevenson's "sedulous ape" (imitating Hazlitt, Lamb, Defoe and others): from memory, unverified `[M]`; can be verified on Gutenberg ("A College Magazine", in *Memories and Portraits*).
- Sentence combining, the one imitation-adjacent method with real trials: effect size 0.50 in Graham & Perin; a college-level experiment (Morenberg et al. 1978) and a 113-pupil seventh-grade study (Pedersen 1978) reported gains in syntactic maturity and quality; fourth-graders with difficulties improved too (Saddler et al. 2008). Syntactic maturity is not the same as storytelling craft. `[F]` ERIC.

**F13. Constraints (Oulipo, Perec).** Rating: **plausible, small direct evidence**.
- Oulipo (founded Nov 1960 by Queneau and Le Lionnais): lipogram, N+7, and other formal rules treated as catalysts. Perec wrote *La Disparition* (A Void) without the letter "e" and used a "story-making machine" for *Life: A User's Manual*. `[F]` https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oulipo
- Experimental support: "The Green Eggs and Ham hypothesis: How constraints facilitate creativity" (Haught-Tromp 2017, Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts; DOI 10.1037/aca0000061) exists (confirmed via Crossref). I could not read its results: details unverified. Stokes' "Creativity from Constraints" (2005) `[M]`, unverified.
- Implication: optional, playful constraint exercises are cheap and fit the philosophy. Do not claim they make writers more creative; claim they give a way to start and a way to practice.

**F14. Writing prompts.** Rating: "prompts as warm-up" **plausible**; "prompts cure blocks / build skill" **popular but weak**; progress-aware process prompts **plausible**.
- ERIC search for studies of creative-writing prompts mostly returned practitioner articles and prompt collections (e.g. "Wild Currents", "500+ Writing Prompts"). Empirical items: Klein, Haug & Arcon (2017), randomized n=73, grades 5-7: rhetorical subgoal prompts increased text quality and complex propositions, content subgoals increased simple propositions. Zielinska et al. (2025), preregistered n=332: prompts tailored to task progress enhanced creative output across design, writing and card tasks, via positive emotion and more time spent. `[F]` ERIC (descriptions only).
- Ahmed & Güss (2022) found blocks disrupt composition more than idea generation (F15), so idea prompts address the minority problem.
- Implication: Parchemin's prompts that ask about the writer's own story (want, wall, turn) are more defensible than a random "write about a lighthouse" generator.

**F15. Writer's block.** Rating: causes/solutions survey **plausible (one survey, self-report)**.
- Ahmed & Güss (2022), 146 writers: four categories (physiological, motivational, cognitive, behavioural); physiological and motivational most prevalent; blocks disrupt composition more than idea generation; most useful strategies: break, a different writing project, forcing oneself to keep writing, discussing ideas; professionals and semi-professionals reported similar blocks and fixes. `[F]` https://api.ies.ed.gov/eric/?search=Ahmad+Guss+Analysis+of+Writer%27s+Block+Causes+and+Solutions&format=json&rows=2 (ERIC record, DOI 10.1080/10400419.2022.2031436).
- Rose's classic claim that rigid rules and inflexible planning cause blocking is supported by case studies and questionnaire work, not experiments. `[F]` ERIC.
- Enriquez & Vaughan (2024) reframe blocking as cognitive, social, cultural, affective and embodied. `[F]` ERIC.
- A "treatments" list on Wikipedia (mindfulness, Pomodoro, freewriting) is weakly sourced; ignore as evidence.

**F16. Freewriting.** Rating: **popular but weak (plausible for fluency and comfort)**.
- Elbow's *Writing Without Teachers* (1973) is a pedagogical argument, not a study. A write-up on "freewriting research" that I checked cites no controlled experiments, only theory plus Boice. `[F]` https://unstoppable.ink/articles/freewriting-research/ (secondary blog, low quality, used only to see what it cites).
- ERIC: mostly essays (Rule 2013 says freewriting is not effortless; Piltch 1979 warns it does not prepare students for planning and editing); empirical items are small classroom studies (Li 2007). `[F]` ERIC.
- Reasonable use: warm-up; not a proven cure.

### 2.5 Teaching writing: the workshop model and alternatives

- Iowa Writers' Workshop founded 1936 under Wilbur Schramm; peer critique in a round-table; the idea was that writing should be technical and rigorous. `[F]` https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iowa_Writers%27_Workshop (the article does not discuss the critiques by McGurl, Bennett or others; I did not verify the oft-repeated Cold War and CIA claims; treat them as `[M]` and contested).
- Critiques found in ERIC (all essays or conceptual pieces, not experiments):
  - Hunley (2002): the workshop was designed for graduate students and fits undergraduate poetry poorly; proposes a "Five-Canon" alternative.
  - Stukenberg (2017): the workshop is a "signature pedagogy" that builds reader awareness and problem solving but can produce overly self-critical thinking.
  - Brown & Brown (1991): workshop as "patriarchal and authoritative"; alternatives are literary models or feminist pedagogy.
  - Kearns (2009): let authors lead discussion and have discussants state their aesthetic standards so judgements do not work as unspoken norms.
  - Casey-Williams (2013): replace "craft" with "know-how" and read for pedagogy.
  - Bogen (1984): process-oriented course built on imitating published authors' methods rather than judging finished product.
  `[F]` ERIC records.
- Salesses, *Craft in the Real World* (Catapult, 2021): examines American workshops and brings in other storytelling traditions (Wikipedia mentions kishotenketsu). `[F]` https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Salesses . His specific claims (craft is culturally situated; a "default reader" assumed by critique; the "gag rule" silencing the author) are from `[M]` memory, unverified in this session; check the book before using them.
- Evidence status: no controlled evidence that workshops produce better writers; school-level evidence supports peer assistance (0.75) and peer feedback (0.58) as general practices, which is not the same thing. Rating: workshop effectiveness is **popular but weak** (unproven); the critiques are **plausible** and mostly qualitative.
- Implication for a solo, offline app: we cannot host a workshop, and we should not reproduce its worst features (public judgement, defaulting to a narrow aesthetic). We can help a writer ask for the feedback they want (author-led questions) and teach them to read their own work.

### 2.6 Effects of tools on thinking

- **Handwriting vs typing, adult note-taking.** Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014; typed notes worse) did not replicate: Urry et al. (2021), 142 participants, found laptop notes had more verbatim words but quiz performance did not differ significantly, and meta-analyses of eight comparable studies did not support longhand improving immediate learning; typed notes may help if there is time to review. `[F]` PubMed 33539228 and https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/writing-notes . Rating: the popular claim is **popular but weak**. This is note-taking in a lecture, not story writing.
- **Handwriting vs typing, word recall.** Mangen et al. (2015), 36 women, within-subjects: better free recall of handwritten words, no difference in recognition. `[S]` https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/view/662 . Rating: **plausible**, small.
- **EEG study.** Van der Weel & Van der Meer (2024; 36 students; 256-channel EEG) found more widespread theta/alpha connectivity for handwriting; a 2025 commentary notes no learning task, single-finger typing that is unnatural, no typing skill data, and statistics that did not test patterns within conditions. `[F]` https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1517235/full . Rating: **popular but weak** as evidence that handwriting is "better for thinking".
- **Children's composing.** Berninger et al. (2009): children in grades 2, 4 and 6 wrote longer essays, faster, by pen than keyboard; grades 4 and 6 wrote more complete sentences by pen. `[S]` https://irvingtonparentsforum.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/comparison-of-pen-keyboard-transcription-modes-in-children-with-without-learning-disabilities-berninger-2009.pdf (listed in search results). Population: children still learning to type.
- **Word processing for weaker writers.** A meta-analysis of 27 studies (grades 1-12) found benefits to quality, length and organisation, with the largest gain in motivation (d = 1.42) and bigger quality gains when programs offered feedback or planning prompts. `[F]` ERIC. Population: weaker school writers.
- **Dictation and speech-to-text.** Both raised text quality and lowered error rates compared with handwriting for elementary English language learners (Arcon, Klein & Dombroski 2017); college students with learning disabilities got higher holistic scores using speech recognition (Higgins & Raskind 1995); students with LD did better with dictation or recognition than with handwriting (MacArthur & Cavalier 2004). `[F]` ERIC. Adult novelists dictating: anecdotal only (Dickens and Henry James dictated at times: `[M]`).
- **Distraction-free modes, word-count display, writing on phones.** I could not find controlled evidence for any of these (my planned ERIC queries on these were cut off by the session limit, so this is "not found", not "does not exist"). Phone writing is described as "an embodied performance" in a 2023 theoretical paper (Schneier, ERIC), not as an effect size. Display of a word count: closest evidence is product goals (0.70) and process goals with progress feedback (Schunk), both in children. Treat word-count display effects on adult fiction as **unknown**.
- Attention residue and task-switching evidence (F6) is the general argument for a calm single-document writing surface.

### 2.7 Idea capture and incubation

- **Incubation meta-analysis.** Sio & Ormerod (2009), 117 studies: positive incubation effects; divergent thinking tasks benefited more than linguistic or visual insight tasks; longer preparation and low-demand activities during the break helped; demanding break tasks reduced the benefit. `[F]` PubMed 19210055. Rating: **well-supported** for lab tasks.
- **Mind wandering.** Baird et al. (2012): an undemanding task during a break produced "substantial improvements" on previously encountered Unusual Uses problems versus demanding tasks, rest, or no break, with gains tied to mind-wandering frequency. `[F]` PubMed 22941876. Rating: **plausible**.
- **Walking.** Oppezzo & Schwartz (2014), four experiments: walking raised divergent-thinking scores (81% of participants improved in study 1), a residual boost remained when seated afterwards, and walking outdoors gave the most novel, highest quality analogies. `[F]` PubMed 24749966. Rating: **plausible**. Extending to story ideas is a leap.
- **Commonplace books.** Practice dates back at least to Seneca's bee metaphor and medieval florilegia; Locke's 1706 method added a systematic index; Emerson, Thoreau, Coleridge and Woolf kept them. `[F]` https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonplace_book . Rating of effect on writing: **anecdotal**.
- **Zettelkasten / Luhmann.** About 90,000 index cards with branching numbering; credited with enabling about 50 books and 550 articles. `[F]` https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zettelkasten . Scholarly account: Schmidt (2016), "Niklas Luhmann's Card Index: Thinking Tool, Communication Partner, Publication Machine" (DOI 10.1163/9789004325258_014, confirmed to exist via Crossref; content unread). No comparative evidence that the method causes productivity. Rating: **popular but weak** as a general productivity claim; **plausible** as a way to keep ideas findable. For fiction writers, a heavy linking system risks becoming a procrastination project: this is my judgement, not evidence.
- **"Sparks" / noticing.** Gaiman: writers get ideas constantly and simply notice. `[S]` Wikiquote. The concept fits the Masicampo finding that offloading a thought into a concrete plan frees attention (F6).

## 3. Existing solutions and tools

All entries below are from my background knowledge `[M]` (unverified in this session, no URLs fetched). Verify before quoting.

- **Scrivener**: binder, corkboard, split view; the long-standing favourite for long fiction; powerful but heavy; structure is optional. Relevant lesson: people want to see the manuscript in pieces.
- **Ulysses, iA Writer, Bear**: minimalist Markdown writing; iA Writer's focus mode dims all but the current sentence/paragraph (Parchemin already has paragraph dimming and typewriter scrolling); iA's "Authorship" feature shades pasted text differently from typed text (an interesting honesty feature).
- **Obsidian, Logseq, Roam, Zettlr**: linked notes (Zettelkasten tradition); local-first Markdown in Obsidian's case. Lesson: backlinks are valued by some and daunting to others.
- **Plottr, Novelcrafter, Reedsy Studio, Dabble, Campfire**: plot grids, story bibles. Novelcrafter and Sudowrite blur into AI generation, which Parchemin rejects.
- **Hemingway-style editors, ProWritingAid, Grammarly**: prose-level critique; risk of flattening voice.
- **750words.com**: private daily 750-word habit with stats; streaks are central (the guilt risk).
- **The Most Dangerous Writing App**: deletes your text if you stop typing for a few seconds; a punitive freewriting tool. Reject the mechanism.
- **Hardware drafters (AlphaSmart, Freewrite)**: single-purpose, no editing screen, enforce drafting separation by hardware.
- **NaNoWriMo**: month-long 50,000-word challenge popularised sprint culture and word-count tracking; I believe the organisation wound down in 2025: unverified `[M]`.
- **Pomodoro/Write-or-Die/Forest-style timers**: sprints with pressure or reward; effects on creative writing quality not established.
- **Offline-first alternatives**: Parchemin's niche is local-first, calm, craft-teaching, no AI ghostwriting, and no account.

## 4. Ideas for Parchemin, ranked

Rankings weigh evidence strength, fit with PHILOSOPHY.md, offline feasibility, build size and risk. Fit questions refer to the six decision questions (start/continue/finish; control; honesty and calm; teaches by use; offline). None of the ideas below needs network access unless noted.

**1. "Leave a note for tomorrow" at the end of a session (S).**
- What: on closing a chapter (or leaving focus mode) offer one optional line: "What happens next?" shown at the top of the chapter the next time you open it, dismissible.
- Evidence: Masicampo & Baumeister (a specific plan removes intrusion and interference); Hemingway's habit; PHILOSOPHY.md already says "start the next day with a sentence already waiting".
- Fit: strong (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q5). Honest: do NOT claim Zeigarnik-style memory benefits. Offline: trivially yes (one field in IndexedDB; include in backup/export only if the writer wants).
- Risk: minimal. Never nag.

**2. Quiet sparks inbox, capture from anywhere in the app (S to M).**
- What: a one-keystroke capture box (a sentence, an image, a voice-note later) that stores to a "sparks" list, optionally attached to a story or a character; nothing forced into structure; a writer can later "plant" a spark as a story idea (the existing story spark: who, want, wall).
- Evidence: commonplace books (long tradition, anecdotal); incubation meta-analysis (ideas arrive during low-demand time, so capture must be instant); Gaiman's "notice". A "resurface a random old spark" button chosen by the writer (never a notification) supports incubation.
- Fit: strong (Q1, Q4, Q6). Conflict: none if it stays unstructured. Size S for text; M with tagging and search.
- Avoid: making this a Zettelkasten with mandatory links.

**3. Optional "just draft" mode (S to M).**
- What: a toggle inside focus mode that turns off spellcheck underlines and word counts, hides toolbar and formatting, and optionally blurs the text already written (keeping the cursor line). A paired "now revise" view puts the draft in read-only form with margin notes (existing snapshots protect the writer: save a snapshot when switching).
- Evidence: working-memory competition (well-supported); separating drafting and editing is plausible but not proven; revision-heavy writers also do well, so never force it. The progress report notes editing loads phonological memory, which suggests a separate revision pass is a different job.
- Fit: strong (Q2 control, Q5 calm). Offline: yes. Honest copy: "Some writers find drafting is easier when they cannot edit. Try it, ignore it."
- Reject: the punitive version (delete text if you stop).

**4. Imitation studio: "copy, hint, rewrite, compare" (M).**
- What: a practice space separate from stories with a small bundled set of public-domain passages (Project Gutenberg texts, with attribution). Modes: copy out slowly (typing or pen), Franklin's method (write brief hints, set aside, rewrite from memory, compare side by side), and "pastiche this paragraph about your own topic". Writers can also paste their own favourite passages for private study (stored locally, flagged as someone else's text so it never ends up in an export of the writer's story).
- Evidence: Franklin's account (verified); reading/modeling effects are modest (study of models 0.25; reading-for-writing 0.44-0.63 in students); the evidence for adult fiction is craft tradition. I'd label it "an old way to learn", not "proven".
- Fit: teaches by use (Q4); the writer's own words only; clean for philosophy. Offline: yes with a bundled corpus (a few hundred KB). Check public-domain status per text and jurisdiction.
- Risk: copyright if users paste modern text (it stays local and private, so fine); make sure exported manuscripts never silently include copied text.

**5. Progress-aware prompts rather than a random prompt generator (S, extension of existing prompts).**
- What: prompts that read the writer's own structure (no cloud, no text analysis beyond local structured fields): e.g. if a chapter has a goal but no conflict, ask "What stops her getting this today?" If a character has a want but no function, ask "What can only he do?" Keep random "spark" prompts as a minor feature labelled as warm-ups, not cures.
- Evidence: prompts tailored to progress (Zielinska 2025, n=332) and rhetorical subgoal prompts (Klein 2017) outperformed no prompts; random idea prompts lack evidence; writer's block is mostly motivational/physiological (F15).
- Fit: very strong (existing craft checks; Q4). Offline: yes (rule-based, local). Size S to M.

**6. Constraint cards, optional and playful (S).**
- What: a short deck of constraints (Oulipo-flavoured): write the scene without using "said"; only dialogue; exactly 100 words; no adjectives; swap the setting's obstacle. Attach to a "warm-up" scratchpad that is never part of the manuscript.
- Evidence: Oulipo tradition (fetched), constraints research (unverified results); at best plausible.
- Fit: good (calm, optional, teaches by use). Offline: yes. Honest copy: "a way in", not a creativity booster.

**7. Rhythm instead of streaks: a self-defined schedule (S).**
- What: let the writer say "I write Tue/Thu/Sat, or 20 minutes when I can"; the home line shows words written this week, never shame. A day off by plan or by life never breaks anything (aligns with the existing "streak that survives an unfinished day").
- Evidence: scheduled sessions beat waiting for inspiration (small Boice experiments); daily is not proven (Sword); habit formation is variable (18-254 days). Process goals with progress feedback (Schunk) in children.
- Fit: strong (Q3 honesty, "gentleness about the habit"). Offline: yes. Size S.

**8. Read-aloud / read-back (S).**
- What: the writer can have a chapter read by the device's voice (the Web Speech API, which depends on the platform and is not guaranteed to work offline on every system; check before building) or read it in a separate "reader" view (a clean page like an e-reader).
- Evidence: Gaiman, Le Guin and others advocate reading aloud (anecdote `[M]`); editing loads phonological memory (progress report), so hearing could help; no controlled evidence in creative writing. Plausible.
- Fit: good. Offline: depends on system voices. Size S (use existing voices only).

**9. Self-chosen feedback: "ask your reader" export (S to M).**
- What: export a chapter as a clean file plus a small card of the writer's own questions ("Where were you bored?", "Whose side were you on?") to hand to a trusted reader; later paste the answers back into notes. The writer controls what is asked, echoing Kearns's author-led critique and avoiding the workshop's worst traits.
- Evidence: feedback matters (adult 0.87, peer 0.58, self 0.62 in school); author-led workshop proposals are untested but reasoned.
- Fit: good (Q2). Offline: yes. Size S to M (builds on export).

**10. Hand-curated "how writers work" notes (S, content only).**
- What: short, honest craft notes quoting named writers with exact sources, labelled as one writer's practice ("Hemingway stopped mid-flow; Butler read widely; others plan on cards"). It shows that methods differ, supporting the "no mandatory structure" principle.
- Fit: Q4, Q3. Risk: misquotation; only use quotes we verified from primary sources.

**11. Lightweight links between sparks, characters and chapters (M, later).**
- A "mentions" feature rather than a full Zettelkasten. Only if writers ask; evidence is anecdotal. Never required.

**12. Dictation capture (L, deprioritise).**
- Evidence is only for learning-disabled and elementary populations; offline speech recognition is unreliable without a bundled model (large). Revisit only if on-device recognition becomes simple.

### Ideas rejected (explicitly)

- **Streak loss, "you missed a day" prompts, daily guilt notifications, red word-count bars, leaderboards, comparison with other writers.** Violate belief #3 and "no guilt gamification". Also, the evidence does not support daily as a universal.
- **Punitive freewriting (text deleted if you pause).** Manufactured urgency; violates calm and control. The evidence for freewriting is weak anyway.
- **Forced drafting/editing separation, forced outlines, forced templates.** The evidence says writers differ (F3).
- **Countdown timers and sprint pressure with social rankings** (NaNoWriMo-style competition features). Manufactured urgency; the writing-quality effects are unmeasured.
- **Analytics on keystrokes, pauses or revision behaviour.** Violates "no data harvesting"; also keystroke features predict quality only weakly (Conijn 2022).
- **AI "improve my prose", auto-complete of sentences, or generated prompts from the writer's text sent to a server.** Ghostwriting and harvesting. (An opt-in, local-only, question-asking helper is a future decision outside this research.)
- **Claims like "handwriting makes you smarter" or "science says write daily".** Overclaiming; see F9 and 2.6.
- **Variable-reward "mystery" prompts or lootbox-style inspiration.** Manipulative dark pattern.

## 5. Open questions and gaps

1. **Is there any controlled evidence on how novelists or poets work, plan or finish?** I found none beyond the expert-poet think-aloud study (n=10 vs 10), interviews and memoir. The bulk of the cognitive literature uses students and essays. Open: a literature search on "fiction writers" and "novelists" with Google Scholar and PsycINFO (not reachable here).
2. **Does showing a word count help or hurt adult creative writers?** Product goals and progress feedback work for school writers; the direction for fiction is unknown, and quantity-versus-quality is a plausible hazard. Parchemin could make the count hideable per session by default.
3. **Do prompts work, and which kind?** The only decent evidence is for progress-tailored and rhetorical subgoal prompts in non-fiction contexts. We lack data on fiction prompts. (A tiny opt-in local A/B is not feasible without analytics, which we refuse.)
4. **Draft-then-edit versus edit-as-you-go for adults:** no direct experiment found. The "invisible writing" literature (typing with the screen blanked) may hold evidence; I did not reach it (unverified, not searched).
5. **Habit evidence in writing:** I could not read the Krashen re-analysis of Boice or the 1983 paper itself, and the Lally paper's finding about missed days was not confirmed. These affect the "streak survives a day off" copy.
6. **Zeigarnik meta-analysis (2025):** reported through Wikipedia only; fetch the primary paper before citing.
7. **Constraints:** the Haught-Tromp 2017 results need reading; Stokes (2005) not verified.
8. **Workshop critique:** Salesses's claims and Chavez's "anti-racist writing workshop" need primary reading; Cold War/CIA claims about Iowa are contested and not verified here. Also McGurl's *The Program Era* (2009), `[M]` unverified.
9. **Professional-writer routines:** Paris Review, Guardian and New Yorker pages were blocked; King, Le Guin, Lamott, Murakami and McPhee details need first-hand checking against the books or accessible transcripts before any quotation.
10. **Distraction-free modes and phone writing:** no controlled evidence located; my planned queries were cut off.
11. **Zettelkasten for fiction:** no evidence beyond Luhmann's output; would linking help story ideas become stories?
12. **On-device speech recognition offline:** feasibility and size for a PWA; not researched.
13. **Translation to children vs adults:** many effect sizes (0.25-0.87) come from grades 1-12; adult transfer is assumed, not shown.

## 7. Summary table: findings by evidence level

**Well-supported**
- Writing processes compete for limited working memory (F1).
- Expert writers revise more and at a more global level than novices (F8; small, old, but consistent).
- Feedback and explicit strategy instruction improve student writing (F10; school populations).
- Making a concrete plan for unfinished goals frees attention (F6).
- Incubation with low-demand breaks helps divergent thinking (2.7; lab tasks).

**Plausible**
- Outlining helps some writers; no single planning style is required (F3).
- Separating drafting from editing eases load (F4).
- Scheduled brief sessions outperform waiting for inspiration (F9).
- Imitation and models help, modestly (F11-F12).
- Constraints can unlock starting (F13).
- Progress-tailored prompts help (F14).
- Walking and mind wandering support idea generation (2.7).
- Dictation helps some groups (2.6).

**Popular but weak**
- "Write every day" as a universal rule (F9).
- Handwriting is better for thinking/learning (2.6).
- Freewriting cures blocks (F16).
- Random prompts cure blocks (F14, F15).
- Stopping mid-sentence works because unfinished tasks stay in memory (F7).
- Zettelkasten boosts productivity (2.7).
- The Iowa-style workshop is the proven way to teach writing (2.5).
- "21 days to a habit" (F9).
- Deliberate practice explains most expert performance (F8).
