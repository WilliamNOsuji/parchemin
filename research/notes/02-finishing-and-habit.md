# 02 - Finishing, habit and abandoned projects

Researcher 2 of 12. Area: why drafts go unfinished, motivation decay, habit formation, streaks, NaNoWriMo, accountability, word-count tools, resuming after a gap, completion statistics.

Method note. About 14 web searches and about 35 page fetches were made before the shared search budget (200 per session) ran out. Several primary papers were PDFs or paywalled pages the fetch tool could not read, so some claims rest on abstracts or on secondary summaries. Every claim carries a confidence label. Anything I could not trace to a source I actually opened is marked **unverified**. No URL below was invented; each appeared in a search result or was fetched.

Confidence key: high = peer-reviewed or primary source I could read; medium = peer-reviewed but only abstract or reliable summary read, or a company/organisation's own account; low = blog, anecdote, or secondary source repeating a number.

---

## 1. Executive summary

1. **No traceable number says what share of people who start a novel finish it.** The famous "97% never finish" and "94% fail" figures have no source I could find (unverified). The only survey with a named sample (ThriftBooks/OnePoll, 2,000 US adults, 2021) says 15% started a book and 8% finished a novel, and people who abandoned said 40% could not work out an ending and 36% lost interest. It is a marketing survey, so confidence is low-medium. Parchemin should never quote a completion statistic.
2. **NaNoWriMo's "win rate" is low and murky.** Figures I found are inconsistent (a secondary source says 35,387 winners from 287,327 participants in 2018 and calls it "nearly 29%", but that arithmetic gives about 12%). Treat any percentage as unverified. What is solid: the nonprofit announced closure on 31 March 2025, citing a six-year decline in participation and money problems, after the 2023 child-safety scandal and the 2024 AI statement.
3. **NaNoWriMo's own surveys (reported by SurveyMonkey) say three things helped people finish: planning, community or an accountability partner, and a strong idea. The first 3 to 4 days mattered a lot, and reaching 10,000 words gave about the same confidence boost as reaching 50,000.** Organisation-reported, medium-low confidence, but it matches Parchemin's "celebrate size" belief.
4. **"Write every day" is weaker science than its fame suggests.** Boice's experiments (brief regular sessions beat binge writing, and reporting output to someone raised it further: 17 vs 64 vs 157 pages a year, as relayed by a secondary page) are widely cited but small and old. Helen Sword (2016, peer-reviewed) argues the evidence is thin and that most successful academics do not write daily. The robust part is regularity plus a low floor, not daily quotas. Note these are academics, not novelists.
5. **If-then planning has the strongest evidence of any tool here.** Gollwitzer and Sheeran's meta-analysis of 94 tests found a medium-to-large effect (d = 0.65) on goal attainment. It is weaker for complex, sustained goals (one secondary summary gives d about 0.31 for exercise; low confidence). Cheap, honest and offline-friendly.
6. **Streaks work and hurt, and the same mechanism does both.** Field and lab studies show an intact streak raises engagement (Silverman and Barasch, 2023, seven experiments; a 60,000-student Peru field trial). The same work finds a broken streak demotivates and can lead people to quit, and that offering ways to repair a streak softens this. Quantifying an activity can also drain enjoyment (Etkin, 2016). This supports Parchemin's existing "streak that survives an unfinished day" and argues for going further.
7. **A missed day does little harm to habit formation; a guilt reaction does more.** Lally et al. (2010): a single missed opportunity had little effect on automaticity, median time to plateau was 66 days with a huge range (18 to 254), and about half of participants had not formed a habit in 84 days. These were eating, drinking and exercise habits, not writing, so copy must stay modest.
8. **Planning for the unfinished is a documented relief.** Masicampo and Baumeister (2011, five experiments) found that making a specific plan for an unfinished goal removed its intrusive effect on attention as well as finishing it did. This supports an "end-of-session note to self" and Hemingway's rule of stopping when you know what happens next (Paris Review, 1958, first-hand).
9. **Accountability has real but modest evidence.** Matthews (Dominican University, conference presentation, 267 people): writing goals plus weekly reports to a friend did best. Shut Up and Write-style sessions raised writing self-efficacy in a small uncontrolled study (24 completers). A trial of writing retreats with a waitlist control reported better doctoral wellbeing (abstract only read via search). None of this needs a server: it needs a way to share a small, content-free progress note.
10. **Big gaps remain.** I found no solid evidence on re-entry after a gap, on burnout in fiction writers, on poetry, or on how word-count tools affect quality. These are stated as open questions, not filled with guesses.

## 2. Problems

### P1. The unmeasured "most drafts never get finished"
- **Description.** Everyone repeats that most people who start a book never finish. No reliable denominator exists: nobody counts starts.
- **Who.** Everyone who writes long form; also affects how apps frame success.
- **Evidence.** ThriftBooks/OnePoll survey of 2,000 US adults (Nov 2021): 15% started a book, 6% reached the halfway point, 8% completed a novel, 24% finished a poem; abandoners cited no ending (40%) and lost interest in story or characters (36%); other barriers were no inspiration (33%), no time (26%), perfectionism (16%). https://studyfinds.com/writing-books-life-worthy-of-deal/ . Skeptical forum posts note the "97%" and "94%" numbers have no source: https://www.royalroad.com/forums/thread/107288?page=1 (seen in search results only; anecdote).
- **Confidence.** Survey: low-medium (commissioned by a retailer, self-report, unclear question wording). The 97%/94% numbers: unverified.
- **Severity.** High as a problem, but the statistic itself is unusable for copy.

### P2. The messy middle: motivation decay and "no ending"
- **Description.** Early excitement fades, the structure gets hard, the writer cannot see how it ends or loses interest in the characters. Draft sits in a drawer.
- **Who.** Novelists and novella writers most; short story writers less.
- **Evidence.** The survey above (no ending 40%, lost interest 36%) is the only quantified signal; low-medium. Philosophy already says "know the last line before you write the first", which targets the first reason. Everything else about the "middle" in the literature I could reach is craft opinion, not data.
- **Confidence.** Low.
- **Severity.** High.

### P3. Big-bang starts that die early (the first days)
- **Description.** The first days decide whether people start habits at all.
- **Who.** New writers, event participants.
- **Evidence.** NaNoWriMo's own surveys (reported by SurveyMonkey, a vendor case study): those who had not started by day 4 or 5 were much less likely to finish, so the organisation moved encouragement earlier. https://www.surveymonkey.com/curiosity/nanowrimo/ . Organisation self-report; no data shown.
- **Confidence.** Medium-low.
- **Severity.** Medium.

### P4. Binge-and-crash writing and the "write every day" overpromise
- **Description.** Writers wait for big blocks of time, binge, then stall. The usual cure, "write every day", is stated more confidently than its evidence allows.
- **Who.** Anyone with a job or family; academics in the studies.
- **Evidence.**
  - Boice, *Professors as Writers* (1990) and related experiments: as relayed by a course page, binge writers produced 17 pages of finished prose a year, brief-regular-session writers 64, and those who also reported output to Boice 157; daily writers also noted far more new ideas. https://www.bmartin.cc/classes/writing.html (secondary summary; I could not read Boice's papers).
  - Sword (2016), *International Journal for Academic Development* 21(4): the "write every day" advice "hangs from a perilously thin research thread"; successful academics' habits are varied and individual. Abstract read at https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1115266 . I could not extract her survey numbers.
- **Confidence.** Boice numbers: medium-low (secondhand, small studies, academic writing). Sword's critique: medium-high (peer-reviewed; abstract only).
- **Severity.** Medium. Implication: Parchemin should favour regular, low-floor sessions over a fixed daily quota, and let the rhythm be the writer's.

### P5. Intentions do not become actions (the intention-action gap)
- **Description.** "I will write more" fails; "after my coffee, at the kitchen table" works much better.
- **Who.** Everyone with a goal.
- **Evidence.** Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) meta-analysis, 94 tests, d = 0.65: https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/10973 (abstract read). A well-known writing-adjacent study (students asked to write a report on Christmas Eve within 48 hours; those who said when and where did far better) is described in Gollwitzer (1999) and Gollwitzer & Brandstatter (1997): https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/gollwitzer_brandstatter_1997_-_implementation_intentions_effective_goal_pursuit.pdf . I could not read the PDFs; a secondary summary gave 32% vs 71% completion, which I mark unverified. Effect is smaller for complex, ongoing behaviours (secondary summary only, low).
- **Confidence.** High that the effect exists; medium on size for long projects.
- **Severity.** High (one of the cheapest levers).

### P6. Slow, uneven habit formation and the "21 days" myth
- **Description.** People expect a habit in 3 weeks; reality is slower and varies widely, so early lapses feel like failure.
- **Who.** Anyone building a writing routine.
- **Evidence.** Lally et al. (2010), 96 people, 84 days: median 66 days to plateau, range 18 to 254; one missed day barely mattered, but many misses lowered the final level; about half had not formed a habit; exercise took about 1.5 times longer than drinking or eating habits. BPS Research Digest summary: https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit . (Original paper page returned 403; the figures come from this digest.) Wood and Runger's review (2016) is the standard source on cues, repetition and context: https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/wood.runger.2016.pdf (PDF unreadable to the tool; not used for claims). The oft-quoted "43% of daily behaviour is habitual" came via a search summary of Wood's 2002 diary study; treat as medium-low.
- **Confidence.** High for Lally's numbers (via digest); low for transfer to writing (a complex creative act).
- **Severity.** Medium.

### P7. Streak design: motivation turns into anxiety, then quitting
- **Description.** A counter that resets to zero turns one bad day into a loss, and some people then abandon the whole practice (the abstinence-violation or "what-the-hell" effect, named from addiction research). Also the streak can become the goal instead of the writing.
- **Who.** Perfectionists, people with irregular lives, people with illness or caring duties.
- **Evidence.**
  - Silverman, Barasch et al. (2023), *Journal of Consumer Research* 49(6), seven experiments: intact streaks raise later engagement; broken streaks reduce it; repair options reduce the damage. https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/49/6/1095/6623414 (abstract read).
  - Etkin (2016), *JCR* 42(6): measurement can reduce enjoyment of an activity and long-term engagement even without rewards. https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/42/6/967/2358309 (abstract read).
  - "Streaking to Success" (Peru, 60,000 students, randomised): highlighting streaks raised platform use and maths scores; authors note streaks can demotivate when a break is outside the learner's control. https://publications.iadb.org/publications/english/document/Streaking-to-Success-The-Effects-of-Highlighting-Streaks-on-Student-Effort-and-Achievement.pdf (403 on direct fetch; summarised from search result and https://www.nber.org/papers/w34173 ).
  - Habitica study "Counterproductive effects of gamification" (Diefenbach, 2019), named on Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitica . I could not open the paper.
  - **Unverified claims seen on blogs, do not use:** "63% more likely to quit after one missed day" (attributed to a "2020 CHI study"); Duolingo streak freeze "+23% retention" (attributed to a 2019 CHI talk). No primary source found.
- **Confidence.** High that streaks cut both ways; low on any specific percentage.
- **Severity.** High for Parchemin because the philosophy rejects guilt mechanics.

### P8. Word-count goals: pressure, false progress and quality
- **Description.** A daily count is easy to measure and rewards volume. It can make a revision day feel like zero, push writers to pad, and turn writing into a chore. NaNoWriMo's own tagline-style instruction was to put quantity over quality (per Wikipedia), which suits a first draft but is awkward for revision.
- **Who.** Perfectionists, revisers, poets, writers with chronic illness or ADHD.
- **Evidence.** Mostly anecdote and design reasoning. The Etkin result (quantification can sap enjoyment) is the nearest solid evidence. I found no study of word-count trackers' effect on prose quality or writer mental health. Pacemaker (launched 2013 by Stuart Young, per a directory page) is a pacing calculator: set a total, a deadline and writing days; it recalculates daily targets. https://www.pacemaker.press/ (page content not readable; features from search results, low).
- **Confidence.** Low.
- **Severity.** Medium. Gap: needs follow-up (see section 5).

### P9. Planning fallacy and unrealistic deadlines
- **Description.** People guess finish dates from the best case and miss them, then feel they failed.
- **Who.** First-time novelists most.
- **Evidence.** Buehler, Griffin and Ross (1994): psychology students estimated 33.9 days for their senior thesis and took 55.5; only about 30% finished within their own estimate. Source: summary at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy (secondary; original not read). Remedies named there: base estimates on past similar projects, break work into parts, and add implementation intentions.
- **Confidence.** Medium.
- **Severity.** Medium. Parchemin's "novel in 30 days" finish line is a stretch target; it must read as an option, not a verdict.

### P10. Re-entry friction: the gap and "where was I?"
- **Description.** After days or weeks away, the writer faces a blank re-read, loses the thread and delays returning.
- **Who.** Anyone with interrupted life; people returning to abandoned drafts.
- **Evidence.**
  - Hemingway (Paris Review, Art of Fiction No. 21, 1958): he reread what he had written each morning and stopped each session while he still knew what would happen next, so he could carry on. First-hand but one writer; anecdote. https://www.theparisreview.org/interviews/4825/the-art-of-fiction-no-21-ernest-hemingway (403 on direct fetch; quote confirmed at https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/sears-eng-1141-course-readings-and-materials/2021/02/04/paris-review-interview-ernest-hemingway-the-art-of-fiction-no-21/ ).
  - Masicampo and Baumeister (2011): writing down a concrete plan for an unfinished goal removed its cognitive intrusion. https://users.wfu.edu/masicaej/MasicampoBaumeister2011JPSP.pdf (PDF unreadable; findings via search summary and abstract-level description, medium). The older "Zeigarnik effect" memory claim does not replicate well (2025 meta-analysis cited on Wikipedia: no memory advantage for unfinished tasks, but a general tendency to resume), so do not use it in copy: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect
- **Confidence.** Low-medium (no direct study of writers resuming).
- **Severity.** High in practice; cheap to address.

### P11. Rigid rules and perfectionism produce blocks
- **Description.** Strict self-imposed rules and fixed plans can freeze a writer.
- **Who.** Students and perfectionist adults.
- **Evidence.** Rose (1980), *College Composition and Communication* 31(4): ten UCLA students, five blocked; blockers followed rigid rules and plans, non-blockers used flexible heuristics. Small sample, 1980. https://publicationsncte.org/content/journals/10.58680/ccc198015931 (abstract level via search). Survey above: perfectionism 16% of barriers (low-medium).
- **Confidence.** Medium-low.
- **Severity.** Medium. It supports "structure is a skeleton, not a cage" and cautions against hard daily quotas.

### P12. Isolation and lost community
- **Description.** Writing alone, with no one expecting anything, makes stopping easy. Community-based events carry risk when the organiser fails.
- **Who.** Solo writers; people who relied on NaNoWriMo's forums.
- **Evidence.**
  - Matthews (Dominican University): 267 participants; written goals alone helped, goals plus weekly progress reports to a friend helped most. Conference presentation, not peer-reviewed: https://scholar.dominican.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1002&context=psychology-faculty-conference-presentations (403; figures via search summary: +42% for written goals, +77% with weekly reports; medium-low). The "70% vs 35%" figure in the same summary is also secondhand.
  - Shut Up and Write!, 12-week pre-post study, 24 completers, no control group; writing self-efficacy (p = 0.020) and strategies (p = 0.041) improved. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-clinical-and-translational-science/article/shut-up-write-builds-writing-selfefficacy-and-selfregulation-in-earlycareer-researchers/EF583441E250770E86EE90719D56C350 (medium, small).
  - Writing-retreat trial on doctoral researchers (100 randomised, waitlist control; reduced distress): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10606499/ (page blocked; claim from search result summary only, medium-low).
  - NaNoWriMo's closure: the nonprofit said it was closing on 31 March 2025 after six years of falling participation; earlier 2023 child-safety failures and a 2024 AI statement ("classist and ableist undertones") caused resignations and sponsor losses. https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/industry-news/publisher-news/article/97466-nanowrimo-nonprofit-shutters.html , https://reactormag.com/nanowrimo-the-nonprofit-organization-is-shutting-down/ , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Novel_Writing_Month
- **Confidence.** Medium for the closure facts; medium-low for the accountability effect sizes.
- **Severity.** Medium-high. The closure created a gap for a calmer, privacy-respecting alternative.

### P13. Burnout and unsustainable pace
- **Description.** Sprint events and large daily targets produce exhaustion and a post-event crash.
- **Evidence.** I found none I could trace beyond anecdotes. Boice's advice was brief sessions that stop before exhaustion (secondhand). Listed as a gap.
- **Confidence.** Low. **Severity.** Unknown.

## 4. Ideas for Parchemin

Philosophy tests used: (1) helps start/continue/finish, (2) writer in control, (3) honest, no guilt, (4) teaches by use, (5) calm, (6) offline, no account. Existing features to build on: `logWords`, streak that survives an unfinished day, today's words and seven-day tally, finish line, milestones, outline audit.

### I1. "Leave a sentence waiting" note (end-of-session handoff)
- **What.** When the writer closes a chapter or leaves focus mode, offer an optional one-line field: "What happens next?" Saved with the chapter. When they return, show it at the top, quietly, with the last paragraph in view.
- **Fit.** Directly implements Philosophy "Habit: start the next day with a sentence already waiting". Backed by Hemingway (anecdote) and Masicampo and Baumeister (medium). Nothing is written for them. Passes all six tests.
- **Offline.** Fully local, one new field in IndexedDB; include in backup JSON.
- **Size.** S.

### I2. "Where was I?" return card
- **What.** On the home screen and on opening a story: last edited chapter, words added last session, the handoff note from I1, a "go to last sentence" button, and an optional "read the last page first" mode. After a long gap, copy stays neutral: "Welcome back. Here is where you left it."
- **Fit.** Addresses P10; no guilt; calm. Do not say "you have been away N days".
- **Offline.** Local only (timestamps already exist).
- **Size.** S.

### I3. Optional "when and where" plan
- **What.** A one-time, skippable card: "When will you write? After what?" Stores a free-text if-then plan and optionally a preferred time of day. Shows it on the home screen as a gentle reminder in-app. Allow edits and deletion.
- **Fit.** Best evidence of any idea here (d = 0.65). No notifications by default; if offered, opt-in and local only. Offline scheduling of web notifications is unreliable in a PWA (not verified; check feasibility before promising).
- **Offline.** Fully local; do not rely on push.
- **Size.** S (UI) or M (if local notifications are explored).

### I4. Rhythm view in place of a chain
- **What.** Replace or complement the streak with "days you wrote this month" and a lifetime total of writing days that never goes down. Keep "a streak that survives an unfinished day" (one or two rest days built in, no purchase, no countdown). Offer "I wrote by hand" or "I thought about the story" as a manual check-in so illness and travel days still count.
- **Fit.** Supports Philosophy "gentleness about the habit". Evidence: broken streaks demotivate, repair options help (Silverman et al.). Quantification can reduce enjoyment (Etkin), so make it quiet and hideable.
- **Offline.** Local.
- **Size.** S to M.
- **Reject:** streak freezes sold or earned by chores, "streak at risk" alerts, lost-streak screens, leaderboards.

### I5. Honest finish-line planner (self-adjusting)
- **What.** Extend the existing finish line: writer enters target length and an end date or none. Parchemin shows words remaining and the pace needed from today, recalculated daily, using neutral language ("at this pace, about 6 weeks"), plus an estimate from the writer's own recent history (reference-class idea). Never "behind". A one-tap "move my finish date" with no penalty.
- **Fit.** Fits "a finish date is a kindness". Planning fallacy research supports using the writer's own history. Conflict risk: the 15 chapters in 15 days and 30 in 30 messages could read as quotas. Offer them as examples, not targets.
- **Offline.** Local, simple arithmetic.
- **Size.** M.

### I6. Goals beyond words
- **What.** Session goals can be minutes, words, lines (poetry), scenes or chapters, or "just open it". Count net words added and also minutes in the editor; revision sessions record time, not just words.
- **Fit.** Poets and revisers are otherwise punished by a word count. Reduces the quantification problem. Rose's findings (flexible beats rigid) point the same way.
- **Offline.** Local; minutes need an idle timer in the editor.
- **Size.** M.

### I7. Tiny floor ("one sentence counts")
- **What.** A "small start" button that opens the story at the last sentence with a 5-minute soft timer and says any amount counts. Based on Tiny Habits and Boice's brief sessions.
- **Fit.** Lowers the bar; no pressure. Evidence is strongest for brief regular sessions, but the "tiny" claim is from health research, so keep copy modest.
- **Offline.** Local.
- **Size.** S.

### I8. Resting shelf, not "abandoned" pile
- **What.** Stories not touched for a while move into a "resting" shelf (writer's choice, never automatic labels like "abandoned"). Each shows its handoff note, word count and one question from the craft tools ("What does your hero want?"). A quiet seasonal prompt on the home screen can offer one resting story, once, with no counter.
- **Fit.** Honours the finishing mission without shame. Answers survey reasons (lost interest, no ending) via craft prompts. Must be opt-in.
- **Offline.** Local.
- **Size.** S to M.

### I9. Middle check-in (optional)
- **What.** At roughly 40 to 60% of the planned length, show an optional card: re-read your story spark, name the wall in one sentence, write the last line if you have not, jump to the chapter you most want to write next. All skippable, nothing generated.
- **Fit.** Addresses P2 using existing craft tools; the "no ending" reason (40%) is from a low-confidence survey.
- **Offline.** Local.
- **Size.** S.

### I10. Content-free progress card for a friend or group
- **What.** "Share this week" makes a short text or image: story title (optional), days written, words, one-line note. No story text. The writer sends it by whatever app they like. A manual "write together" sprint timer with start, silent writing, and share-out, usable in a room or on a call.
- **Fit.** Accountability helps (Matthews, SUAW; modest evidence). Respects no accounts and no harvesting: nothing is uploaded unless the writer shares it.
- **Offline.** Local; uses the OS share sheet or copy-to-clipboard.
- **Size.** S (card) and S to M (sprint timer).

### I11. Milestone for 10,000 words
- **What.** Add a gentle milestone at 10,000 words and note it with an honest line ("ten thousand words is more than most people ever write on one story"). Careful: do not claim that last sentence without evidence; use "ten thousand words is a lot of sentences".
- **Fit.** "Celebration of size". Based on the NaNoWriMo survey that 10,000 words gave a similar confidence lift to 50,000 (organisation report, medium-low).
- **Offline.** Local.
- **Size.** S.

### I12. First-days welcome
- **What.** A gentle new-story flow that gets some words down in the first session (spark, then a first paragraph), with the "when will you write again?" prompt from I3 right after.
- **Fit.** Targets P3. Do not create fake urgency ("Day 2, don't stop!").
- **Size.** S.

### Rejected ideas (manipulative or guilt-based)
- **Reject:** Habitica-style penalties (health loss, character death), "your streak is about to end" notifications, streak freezes as a monetised item, leaderboards or public rankings, comparison with other writers' counts.
- **Reject:** monster-battle word races that reward volume over meaning (4thewords-style), unless wholly opt-in and framed as play.
- **Reject:** any copy quoting a completion statistic ("97% never finish") or a habit statistic ("21 days") as fact.
- **Reject:** auto-labelling stories "abandoned", or showing days since last edit as a red number.
- **Caution:** daily default quotas (e.g. 1,667 words). Offer as an example, never the default.

## 5. Open questions and gaps for a follow-up round

1. **Re-entry research.** I found no study of writers resuming after a gap. Search task-resumption and interruption research (cognitive psychology), journaling "next action" practices, and writers' own accounts (Murakami, King, Lamott, Cameron). Test whether a handoff note is better than re-reading.
2. **Word-count trackers and quality.** No direct evidence found on effects on prose or mental health. Look for surveys of Scrivener, Pacemaker or 4thewords users, forum threads on "word count anxiety", and any research on quantity vs quality goals in writing instruction (for example Boice's later work, Kellogg's writing research).
3. **Primary-source NaNoWriMo numbers.** Official participant and winner counts by year (nanowrimo.org blog archives were unreachable) and what Camp NaNoWriMo and community successors exist now. Check the 2018 and 2019 figures flagged above. Also check whether any independent study exists on winners vs non-winners.
4. **Primary Boice and Sword data.** Boice's sample sizes and design, and Sword's survey numbers (the PDF text could not be extracted). Needed before quoting any number about brief daily sessions.
5. **Burnout and sustainable pace in creative writers.** Nothing traced. Also poetry and short-form completion, and neurodivergent writers (ADHD, executive function, chronic illness) and how rigid habit tools affect them.
6. **Does if-then planning work for long creative projects?** The meta-analysis is broad; the effect on multi-month projects is smaller in one secondary source. Look for studies on thesis or dissertation completion with implementation intentions.
7. **Offline feasibility of local reminders in a PWA.** Check Notification Triggers and Periodic Background Sync status and iOS limits before offering any reminders.
8. **Scrivener targets, Draft2Digital goals, Written? Kitten! and Write or Die.** Not examined; read manuals and user complaints.
9. **Streak studies in creative domains.** Silverman et al. used consumer and fitness settings; find any writing-specific evidence.
10. **Accountability without a server.** Is a manual share card enough? Look at what write-together communities actually use (Discord sprints, Shut Up and Write chapters).
