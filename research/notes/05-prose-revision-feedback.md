# 05. Prose craft, revision and feedback

Researcher 5 of 12. Scope: common prose problems, how revision works, self-editing, feedback cultures, tools and their limits, version control for writers, and the research literature.

Method note. About 45 searches and fetches were made before the shared web-search budget ran out (the session cap of 200 searches was hit, so the last few topics were fetched directly instead of searched). Many fetches returned a model-written summary of the page, not the raw text, so quotes are short and only used where the summary reproduced them. Several PDFs (Pullum, Lerman one-pager, an APA article) and several paywalled pages could not be read. Anything not read first-hand is labelled `unverified` or `search-result only`. Confidence labels: HIGH = primary source or peer-reviewed and read; MED = credible secondary or practitioner source read; LOW = anecdote, vendor page, or search snippet only.

## 1. Executive summary

1. **Revision is not one job.** Sommers (1980) found experienced writers treat revision as recursive, reorganising meaning throughout drafting, while students mostly swapped words and fixed surface errors. Flower & Hayes (1981) say the same of writing generally: planning, translating and reviewing nest inside one another. A tool that presents revision as a single linear checklist will not match how writers work. (HIGH on the existence and thrust of both papers; the original texts were not read, only abstracts and secondary summaries.)
2. **Working memory is the bottleneck.** Kellogg's model holds that planning, translating and reviewing all draw on one limited executive resource; expertise comes from reducing the load of each so attention is free for higher-level work (Kellogg 2008, JoWR). Design implication: revision tools should reduce what the writer must hold in their head (maps, lists, one question at a time), not add more.
3. **"Where do I start?" is the most documented revision pain.** Practitioner sources repeatedly name tackling everything at once, line edits before structure, and conflicting feedback as the causes of overwhelm, and recommend passes ordered structure, then scene, then sentence. This is craft consensus, not experiment (MED).
4. **Rule-based style checkers produce mostly false positives for fiction.** Reviews of Hemingway Editor, Grammarly and ProWritingAid agree that they cannot tell a flabby sentence from a deliberate one, and flag voice as error. One editor reported "twenty-odd or more" unnecessary flags per useful one. The rules they enforce (adverbs, passive, filter words, "said") are real craft heuristics, but each has well-documented legitimate exceptions. Pullum shows the passive "rule" is often applied to sentences that are not passive at all. (MED-HIGH; evidence is reviews and linguistics argument, not controlled studies of writers.)
5. **Readability scores are weak proxies.** Flesch-Kincaid style formulas count sentence and word length and ignore coherence, organisation and reader knowledge. Wikipedia's summary of the literature says most experts consider them highly misleading when used as targets. Shortening sentences to hit a score can make text harder to read. (MED.)
6. **Feedback is high-value and high-risk.** Meta-analytic evidence in school settings says feedback improves writing (adult 0.87, self 0.62, peer 0.58, computer 0.38 effect sizes, grades 1-8; Graham, Hebert & Harris 2015), but that is students, not novelists. For adult creative writers, evidence is first-hand accounts: premature critique derails drafting, groups pile on after the first negative voice, novices diagnose badly, and a few writers say they stopped writing. Structured protocols (Milford silence rule, Lerman's Critical Response Process) exist specifically to protect the writer's intent. (MED, mostly anecdote.)
7. **Reading aloud works, with direct evidence.** An experimental study (JARMAC; authors not retrieved) found proofreading aloud improved detection of both typos and contextual errors over silent reading, and people did not expect it to. Distance from the draft (Gaiman's "put it in a drawer") is universal craft advice with little experimental backing found. (MED-HIGH for aloud; LOW for rest period as measured effect.)
8. **Writers fear losing text, and that fear shapes revision.** Scrivener's snapshots exist for exactly this; documented limits are per-document scope, no whole-project view, text-only, destructive rollback, and compare only on macOS (per two user/author sources). Parchemin's snapshots already cover the basics, but have no compare and snapshots are deleted when their scene is deleted.
9. **The best fit for Parchemin is "questions and maps, never fixes".** Reverse outline, a personal watch-list of the writer's own crutch words, read-aloud playback, a cuts drawer, snapshot compare and a feedback-request note all support judgment and need no network.
10. **Anything that rewrites sentences, scores prose with a single grade, or says "fix" is rejected.** It conflicts with PHILOSOPHY.md, and the evidence says it also performs badly.

## 2. Problems

Format per problem: description, who it affects, evidence, confidence, severity (for a writer finishing a book). Severity is my judgment.

### 2.1 Revision overwhelm and not knowing where to start
- **Description.** A finished draft feels like one undifferentiated mass of problems. Writers either freeze, polish sentences in chapters that may be cut, or revise forever without a plan.
- **Affects.** First-novel writers especially; anyone with a long draft.
- **Evidence.** Elle LaMarca (Writers.com) lists causes: treating revision as "fixing mistakes", doing everything at once, line edits before structure, too much unfiltered feedback; recommends phases, small weekly goals and a one-to-three-month break (https://writers.com/feature/rewriting-your-novel-how-to-approach-revisions-without-overwhelm). Sol Stein's "triage" idea (fix major problems first, as a developmental editor would) is summarised in search results only (https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/186004.Stein_on_Writing, unverified detail). Sommers' finding that students equate revision with word-level edits is consistent (https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ240356, abstract read).
- **Confidence.** MED (consistent practitioner consensus; no controlled study found).
- **Severity.** HIGH. This is where drafts get abandoned.

### 2.2 Writers cannot see their own draft (familiarity blindness)
- **Description.** Authors read what they meant, not what is on the page, so they miss gaps, repetitions and unclear passages.
- **Evidence.** Gaiman: print it, put it away, then read it "as if you've never read it before" (https://www.neilgaiman.com/FAQs/Advice_to_Authors). Writers.com: revising too soon means seeing what you want to be there. A PubMed record on error detection and familiarity exists (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15248593) but the page did not load; its findings are unverified. Reading aloud measurably helps (see 2.3).
- **Confidence.** MED for the phenomenon; LOW for quantified benefit of resting.
- **Severity.** HIGH.

### 2.3 Silent reading misses errors; reading aloud helps
- **Evidence.** Study "Reading Aloud Improves Proofreading (but Using Sans Forgetica Font Does Not)", Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition: aloud beat silent for typos and contextual errors; participants did not predict the benefit (reported in a search result summary; the ResearchGate and Ovid pages returned 403/402, so I did not read the paper: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359306925_Reading_aloud_improves_proofreading_but_using_Sans_Forgetica_font_does_not). Gaiman describes reading aloud revealing a book's narrative voice (https://journal.neilgaiman.com/2009/07/day-2-of-ala-and-reading-things-aloud.html).
- **Confidence.** MED-HIGH for effect on proofreading; LOW that it extends to developmental problems (the study is about errors).
- **Severity.** MED.

### 2.4 Structural problems are invisible from inside the scene
- **Description.** Pacing, subplot dropouts, POV balance, scenes that do no work, and continuity drift can't be seen while reading line by line.
- **Evidence.** Reverse outlining (outline after drafting from what is actually on the page) is a widely taught fix. Mary Kole describes read-and-note, group, structure (https://www.marykole.com/reverse-outline). Katey Schultz colour-codes POV narrator and separates present scenes, summary and flashback, and found she had never physically described some characters (https://kateyschultz.com/2014/02/revising-novel-reverse-outlining/). Both are practitioner accounts.
- **Confidence.** MED (first-hand craft practice; no trials).
- **Severity.** HIGH for novels, LOW for short pieces.

### 2.5 Show vs tell, and its misuse
- **Description.** "Show, don't tell" is a useful correction for flat, summarised emotion and a harmful absolute when applied to every sentence.
- **Evidence.** Wikipedia notes the phrase is commonly attributed to Chekhov though he wrote about "small details" in nature description; it also records counterarguments from James Scott Bell and Orson Scott Card that telling moves a story fast, and Viet Thanh Nguyen's argument that the rule disadvantages writers whose readers need explicit explanation (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Show,_don%27t_tell, read; individual claims within it unverified against primary sources). Browne & King's "Resist the Urge to Explain" targets redundant explaining of what a scene already shows (blog summary: https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/7076214-resist-the-urge-to-explain; the book itself was not read).
- **Confidence.** MED.
- **Severity.** MED. The risk is mechanical application, which a checker would encourage.

### 2.6 Filter words and distance
- **Description.** Verbs like saw, felt, noticed, realised, wondered can hold the reader at arm's length; but they also carry deliberate effects (realisation, uncertainty, retrospective narrators).
- **Evidence.** Louise Harnby (proofreader): filter words can "enrich a novel" when intentional but otherwise create distance (https://www.louiseharnbyproofreader.com/blog/filter-words-in-fiction-purposeful-inclusion-and-dramatic-restriction). TD Storm lists three cases where filtering is justified: analytical narration, realisations, and perception-as-tension (https://stormwritingschool.com/filtering/). Close narration relates to free indirect style, used consistently since Austen and Goethe per Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_indirect_speech).
- **Confidence.** MED.
- **Severity.** LOW-MED. It is a style dial, not a defect.

### 2.7 Adverbs, passive voice and other "rules"
- **Adverbs.** King's "road to hell is paved with adverbs" is a heuristic about telling the reader what to feel (quoted in search results; see https://www.novlr.org/the-reading-room/the-road-to-hell-is-paved-with-adverbs). Novlr notes that Ben Blatt's book *Nabokov's Favorite Word Is Mauve* finds outliers among celebrated authors; I could not read Blatt's own text or numbers, so none are recorded (the Wikipedia page for it 404'd).
- **Passive voice.** Geoffrey Pullum argues the English passive is widely misidentified, including by Strunk & White, whose own examples of "passive" sentences are often not passive (search-result summaries of https://www.lel.ed.ac.uk/~gpullum/passive_loathing.pdf and the Chronicle/NPR pieces, e.g. https://www.chronicle.com/article/whos-afraid-of-strunk-and-white/; PDF unreadable, exact counts not retrieved, so unverified). Consequence for tools: a grammar-based passive flag will be right more often than a writer's intuition, yet "passive = bad" remains wrong as advice.
- **Said-bookisms.** Wikipedia records King favouring "said" and Cory Doctorow calling fancy tags "writerly laziness" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Said-bookism). A craft opinion, not a finding.
- **Confidence.** MED (heuristics), HIGH that they have legitimate exceptions.
- **Severity.** LOW individually. Dangerous in aggregate when writers obey every rule and flatten voice (Anne R. Allen calls this "The Writing Rules Police", https://annerallen.com/2022/02/10-dangerous-critiques/).

### 2.8 Info-dumps and "As you know, Bob"
- **Description.** Exposition delivered in a lump, or as dialogue between characters who already know the facts.
- **Evidence.** Craft consensus in several blog sources; the "As you know, Bob" name and the questions "does the character already know this? does the reader need it yet?" appear in the results (e.g. https://helenhawkinseditorial.co.uk/as-you-know-bob-how-to-spot-and-avoid-exposition-and-the-dreaded-info-dump/, search-result snippet only).
- **Confidence.** MED-LOW (no depth read).
- **Severity.** MED, especially for fantasy, SF and historical writing.

### 2.9 POV slips, head-hopping, tense drift
- **Description.** Unintended shifts of viewpoint or tense within a scene. Shifts themselves are legitimate technique (multiple POV, historical present).
- **Evidence.** Wikipedia's narrative-mode page treats multiple POV and tense choices as accepted techniques with published examples and is silent on prohibitions (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative_mode). Schultz's colour-coded POV map is one human-run detection method (see 2.4). No study of POV-slip frequency found.
- **Confidence.** LOW for prevalence; HIGH that it is a real, commonly edited issue (editors name it constantly) but I have no primary source on how often.
- **Severity.** MED. Easy to fix once seen; hard to see.

### 2.10 Pet words, crutch phrases and repetitive rhythm
- **Evidence.** AutoCrit advertises detection of repeated words and user-defined crutch phrases (https://www.autocrit.com/, vendor page). ProWritingAid's Echoes and Sentence Length reports target the same thing (described in https://loreteller.com/learn/grammarly-vs-prowritingaid/, vendor-affiliated blog but reasonably balanced). Repetition detection is mechanical and reliably computable, unlike "show vs tell". The ProWritingAid reviewer rates it good at "unnecessary thats and repeated words" (https://www.groundcreweditorial.com/blog/prowritingaid-review-2026).
- **Confidence.** MED.
- **Severity.** MED. It is the most automatable and least controversial item in this list.

### 2.11 Weak or stilted dialogue
- **Evidence.** Treated by Browne & King and Stein (titles only, books not read); McKee's *Dialogue* is already a cited source in PHILOSOPHY.md. The "cover the names" test is already on Parchemin's roadmap.
- **Confidence.** LOW (no sources read in depth).
- **Severity.** MED-HIGH.

### 2.12 Purple prose
- **Evidence.** Wikipedia: no precise rule exists, judgment is subjective, term traces to Horace's "purple patches" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_prose). Not computable with any reliability.
- **Confidence.** HIGH that it cannot be rule-detected.
- **Severity.** LOW-MED.

### 2.13 Readability scores mislead
- **Evidence.** Wikipedia's Readability page: formulas use sentence length and word length; "most experts agree" simple formulas "can be highly misleading"; simplifying by length alone may make text harder (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Readability, read; underlying citations not checked). Reviews of Hemingway Editor note it uses grade level as its main measure, penalises long or rhythmic sentences and "cannot recognize voice, rhythm, or intentional style" (https://blog.chapter.pub/hemingway-editor/ and search snippets for https://originality.ai/blog/hemingway-editor-plus-readability-review, https://proofed.com/knowledge-hub/hemingway-editor-vs-human-editor-a-comparison/; the latter two search-snippet only).
- **Confidence.** MED-HIGH.
- **Severity.** MED. Mostly an argument for what not to build.

### 2.14 Harmful or useless feedback
- **Description.** Critique that attacks the writer, comes too early, pulls in conflicting directions, or comes from non-peers.
- **Evidence.**
  - Anne Beall (Substack) describes group pile-on after a first dismissal, leader authority, novice evaluators, a member told she "wasn't a good writer" who "never wrote again" (anecdote, secondhand) (https://annebeall.substack.com/p/is-your-critique-group-quietly-destroying).
  - Jane Friedman lists failure modes: mismatch between group and project stage, workshopping while drafting stalls forward motion, conflicting feedback, unequal reciprocity, skill mismatch (https://janefriedman.com/why-your-amazing-writing-group-might-be-failing-you/). Rachel Neumeier's summary of Friedman adds that novices judge other novices poorly and small slices hide structural problems (https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/15403250-the-dangers-of-critique-groups).
  - Anne R. Allen's ten "dangerous critiques" (realism brigade, grammar enforcers, genre dogmatists, soul-crushers, writing-rules police and others) (https://annerallen.com/2022/02/10-dangerous-critiques/).
  - Anne Janzer on lingering emotional damage and an expressive-writing recovery exercise (https://annejanzer.com/destructive-feedback/); her use of Pennebaker's research is hers, not verified here.
  - Viet Thanh Nguyen: the workshop can be "a hostile place for women and people of color" and assumes a default reader (https://vietnguyen.info/2017/viet-thanh-nguyen-reveals-writers-workshops-can-hostile). Junot Diaz's "MFA vs. POC" makes a related argument (search-result summaries only, e.g. https://remezcla.com/culture/junot-diaz-mfa-vs-poc-a-response/).
- **Confidence.** MED (several independent first-person accounts; no prevalence data).
- **Severity.** HIGH for the writers affected; unknown frequency.

### 2.15 Fear of losing text, and "killing darlings"
- **Description.** Cutting feels like destruction, so writers keep bloat or lose nerve; or they cut, regret it, and cannot recover the passage.
- **Evidence.** The phrase comes from Arthur Quiller-Couch: "Murder your darlings", about deleting self-indulgent "exceptionally fine writing" (*On the Art of Writing*, lectures 1913-14, per https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Quiller-Couch; Wikipedia suggests a possible earlier source in Meredith, 1859; I did not check the primary text). King's "second draft = first draft minus 10%" and "murdering children" are secondhand in a search summary (https://signalvnoise.com/posts/322-excerpts-from-stephen-kings-on-writing, not fetched). A writer-blog account frames Scrivener snapshots as the answer to the instinctive fear of losing work (https://blog.bacildonovanwarren.com/2018/08/save-me-backing-up-scrivener-projects.html). I found no study of cut-file use; the "cuts file" idea is practitioner folk practice. My searches for it were blocked by the search cap, so this is a gap, not a finding of absence.
- **Confidence.** MED for the fear and phrase history; LOW for cuts-file prevalence.
- **Severity.** MED.

### 2.16 First-draft perfectionism spilling into revision confusion
- **Evidence.** Lamott's "shitty first drafts" (draft as raw material, be kind to yourself) is the standard counter (https://www.scribophile.com/academy/what-is-a-shitty-first-draft, secondary summary). Lev Raphael publicly disagrees with Lamott (title seen in search results: https://www.levraphael.com/blog/why-i-dont-quote-anne-lamott/; page returned a 500 so his argument is unread). King's "closed door" first draft, "open door" second is secondhand. Zadie Smith and others offer permission-giving lines (search snippets only).
- **Confidence.** MED that this is common advice; no data on effect.
- **Severity.** MED.

## 4. Existing solutions and tools

### 4.1 Human methods (strong evidence of use, little controlled evidence)
- **Reverse outline.** Cheap, scene-by-scene map of what the draft does. Kole, Schultz and others use it (see 2.4).
- **Passes by level.** Structure, then scenes, then sentences, then proof (Writers.com; Stein's triage).
- **Rest the draft.** Gaiman: drawer. Writers.com: one to three months.
- **Read aloud / print / change format.** Aloud: JARMAC study. Print and format change: practitioner claim only, no source read.
- **Closed-door first draft.** King, secondhand.
- **Reader-first feedback frameworks.**
  - *Milford method* (founded 1956 by Damon Knight, Judith Merril, James Blish; per Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milford_Writer%27s_Workshop): manuscripts read in advance, writer silent while each person gives an uninterrupted critique (Wikipedia says four minutes), then the writer responds. Clarion descends from it (search summary only).
  - *Liz Lerman's Critical Response Process* (1990): statements of meaning, artist as questioner, neutral questions from responders, opinions last and only by invitation (https://www.tools-not-rules.com/blog/2016/9/14/feedback-liz-lermans-critical-response-process; Writer's Digest version https://www.writersdigest.com/craft-technique/4-steps-to-useful-critiques-the-lerman-method). It was built because artists tended to apologise rather than ask questions.
  - *Iowa-style workshop* and its critics: Mark McGurl and Eric Bennett (*Workshops of Empire*) argue the workshop shaped a particular aesthetic (search-result summary, https://uipress.uiowa.edu/books/workshops-empire; not read). Nguyen and Diaz add that the default reader is unmarked.
- **Roles.** Wikipedia defines alpha readers (unrevised drafts or no ending), beta readers (revised drafts, reader's point of view), critique partners (writers) and sensitivity readers; it is a stub and the roles are conventions, not standards (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_reader).

## 5. Ideas for Parchemin

Test key (from PHILOSOPHY.md): Q1 helps a story that is theirs; Q2 writer stays in control; Q3 honest; Q4 teaches by use; Q5 calm; Q6 offline, no account. Build size: S under a week, M one to three weeks, L over a month. Existing assets: `Snapshot` store (`id, sceneId, label, content, words, createdAt`) in `src/types.ts`, `takeSnapshot` in `src/db.ts`, `SnapshotsPanel` in `src/components/Panels.tsx`, outline audit in `src/craft/audit.ts`, per-chapter plan fields (goal, conflict, turn, POV, setting, prop).

### 5.1 Reverse outline view (write-after-draft map)
Per scene, the writer (not the app) types one line: "what happens" and "what changes", with a POV tag and a status. Shown beside the planned chapter plan, so plan-vs-reality gaps are visible. Optional pre-fill of POV from the chapter plan.
- **Fit.** Strong. Q1, Q2, Q4 (it teaches the technique by doing it). Extends "the outline audit reads the whole book back to you".
- **Conflict.** None, provided the app never writes the summaries. An automatic summary of the writer's scenes would be a reject.
- **Offline.** Fully. **Size.** M (new fields on `Scene`, one view).
- **Evidence.** Kole, Schultz (practitioner).

### 5.2 "Revision passes" as optional questions, not a checklist
A gentle, skippable panel offering a pass by level: whole book, scene, sentence, proof. Each pass lists two or three questions and shows only that level's tools (map, goals and turns, then watch-list, then read-aloud). The writer may order or ignore passes. The panel should say that revision is recursive (Sommers) and that going back is normal.
- **Fit.** Directly answers "where do I start?" while honouring Q2 and "structure is a skeleton". Must be off by default or tucked away (Q5, "no mandatory structure").
- **Offline.** Yes. **Size.** S-M (copy and layout).
- **Risk.** A checklist can feel like homework; word it as questions. Do not display "revision progress" bars that shame.

### 5.3 The writer's own watch-list (crutch words)
The writer types words and phrases they know they overuse (just, suddenly, "let out a breath"). The app only counts and highlights them and shows distance between repeats, in the writer's own list, and offers nothing in return. No built-in "bad word" list. Optionally show an "echo" view: same uncommon word within N words. Dialect and intentional repetition are the writer's call: a per-word "keep" mark.
- **Fit.** Good: it automates what is mechanical and leaves judgment to the writer. Passes Q2 and Q3 as long as copy says "you asked me to watch these".
- **Conflict.** A shipped default list of "weak" words would encode the rules-police problem (2.7); avoid. No scores.
- **Offline.** Yes (local text scan). **Size.** S-M (Tiptap decoration plugin).
- **Evidence.** Repetition is the best-supported use of ProWritingAid and AutoCrit; false positives are low when the list is the writer's own.

### 5.4 Read-aloud playback (device voices)
Play a scene using the browser's `speechSynthesis`, with the current sentence highlighted, a speed control and pause. Optionally "read to a metronome" with no highlight, so the ear does the work. Offer a hint to read it yourself aloud when no local voice exists.
- **Fit.** Direct experimental support for reading aloud (JARMAC). Passes all six if kept to local voices and no cloud TTS.
- **Offline.** Depends on OS voices; test on Android Chrome, iOS Safari, desktop. If a platform only offers network voices, the feature must hide itself, not silently send text anywhere. **Size.** S-M.
- **Caveat.** TTS voices flatten prose rhythm, so say "this is a read-through, not a verdict".

### 5.5 Snapshot upgrades: compare, notes, whole-story snapshot, protect from deletion
- Side-by-side or inline diff between a snapshot and the current text (additions underlined, cuts struck through, as Scrivener does for individual documents).
- A one-line "why I did this / what I plan to change" note per snapshot (Scrivener supports annotations).
- A "snapshot the whole story" action: one name, all chapters.
- A first fix, small and important: `db.ts` deletes a scene's snapshots when the scene is deleted (lines around 111-113) and deletes them with the story. Consider moving deleted scenes and their snapshots into a "Drawer" (5.6) before removal, or warn when snapshots exist.
- Also noted (not my area): `SnapshotsPanel` renders stored HTML via `dangerouslySetInnerHTML`; backup restore merges content from a file, so imported HTML should be sanitised. Flag for the security/tech researchers.
- **Fit.** Q1, Q2, Q3; supports "safe to finish".
- **Offline.** Yes (diff in browser, e.g. a small library or hand-written word diff). **Size.** M (diff), S each for note, whole-story snapshot.

### 5.6 The Drawer: a cuts file that cuts into
When the writer deletes a block of text, offer (never force) a "Keep in the drawer" step: selected text goes into a per-story Drawer list with its source chapter and date, and can be dragged back. Selecting a passage and choosing "Set aside" does the same. Also accepts whole scenes.
- **Fit.** Addresses the fear of losing text (2.15), makes cutting feel reversible, and fits "the writer owns the work". Quiet language: "set aside", not "deleted".
- **Conflict.** None; keep the drawer private and exportable (export in Markdown, backup in JSON, drawer inside both).
- **Offline.** Yes. **Size.** M.
- **Evidence.** Cuts-file practice is folk; the fear is documented. Say it is a belief about relief, not a statistic.

### 5.7 A feedback-request note and a "how to read feedback" companion
For when the writer shares with someone: a printable or exportable one-page cover note with the writer's own questions (Lerman step 2: "what do you want to know?") and a short, kindly worded guide for the reader (what you felt, where you got lost, where you skimmed; no rewriting). Pair with a private "feedback log": paste comments, tag each as "pattern" (several readers), "mine to weigh", or "not for this book", and decide per comment. Offers a quiet place to compare readers without sending anything anywhere.
- **Fit.** Directly counters the documented harms (premature critique, conflicting feedback, mismatch of needs; Friedman, Writers.com "look for patterns across readers"). It never judges the work.
- **Conflict.** Avoid any in-app multi-user critique (accounts, network, moderation; Q6). Sharing is by export or print.
- **Offline.** Yes. **Size.** S (export template) and M (feedback log).
- **Evidence.** Lerman, Milford, Friedman, Allen, Beall (practitioner).

### 5.8 Dialogue cover-the-names test (already on roadmap)
Show a scene's dialogue with speaker names hidden and ask the writer to guess who speaks; no scoring, no judgments. Already planned; research adds only that it fits "voice" craft and that no checker does this. **Size.** M.

### 5.9 Lightweight "scene one-liner" and POV tag as reverse-outline seed
The chapter plan already has POV and tense could be added as a manual tag per scene; the app can show a coloured POV strip across the book, like Schultz's colour coding, and flag nothing. **Size.** S. Teaches POV by seeing the pattern.

### 5.10 Resting-the-draft ritual (soft)
After "finish line" is reached, offer, once, a note: "Many writers set a draft aside for a while. You can mark the date you will come back." A "return date" stored locally; at most a quiet line on the story card when the day arrives, never a push notification, never a lost-streak mechanic. **Fit.** Matches craft consensus (Gaiman, Writers.com); evidence for benefit is thin so word it as practice, not proof. **Size.** S.

### 5.11 Rejected or constrained
- **Reject: auto-fix, "improve this sentence", AI rewrite suggestions, grammar auto-correct of fiction.** Conflict with "never ghostwrite"; also reviews show these flatten voice (Hemingway Plus AI "fixes", ProWritingAid Sparks).
- **Reject: readability grade or a single "quality score" for prose.** Evidence 2.13; invites writing to the metric (Q3, Q4).
- **Reject: showing "passive voice" or "adverb" counts as defects by default.** If ever offered, make it opt-in, framed as a question ("this sentence uses the passive: is that what you want here?"), and based on real grammatical parsing, not S&W-style guesses. I would not build it in the first release; the writer's own watch-list (5.3) covers the legitimate cases.
- **Reject: crowd or community critique feature, public sharing, upvoting.** Conflicts with local-first, no accounts, calm.
- **Reject: AI "beta reader" that gives opinions on the story as if it were a human reader,** unless it is later opt-in, labelled, questions-only (PHILOSOPHY.md allows "questions and critique" opt-in). Even then, evidence of its usefulness is missing here, and the arXiv search results I saw were not read.

### 5.12 Suggested order
1. Snapshot protection and compare (5.5) and the Drawer (5.6). They are the cheapest way to make revision feel safe.
2. Reverse outline with POV strip (5.1, 5.9). It is the best-evidenced structural aid.
3. Read-aloud (5.4) and the writer's watch-list (5.3).
4. Feedback-request note and log (5.7), then the optional pass questions (5.2).

## 6. Open questions and gaps

1. **Is there any controlled evidence for revision aids on adult creative manuscripts?** I found none. All fiction evidence is practitioner craft or anecdote. Should the app avoid any claim of effectiveness? (Probably yes, per "do not overclaim".)
2. **Pullum's actual counts** (how many of Strunk & White's passive examples are passive) were not retrieved; the PDF could not be read. Needs a second pass before any copy cites it.
3. **Blatt's adverb data** (*Nabokov's Favorite Word Is Mauve*) not read; do not cite numbers.
4. **The 2026 peer-feedback meta-analysis** link was 403 and is unverified. The Graham et al. numbers are for grades 1-8 only.
5. **Reading-aloud study details** (authors, sample size) were not retrieved. A summary reported the direction, not the numbers.
6. **No primary source read** for Sommers or Flower & Hayes full texts, Browne & King, Stein, Lamott, King's *On Writing*, or Lerman's book. Quotes about them came from secondary pages.
7. **Cuts-file practice and prevalence**: searches were cut off by the search cap. Worth a follow-up by another researcher (writer interviews, forum posts).
8. **Version control for writers beyond Scrivener**: Word/Docs version history, git-for-writers, and whether writers actually use older versions after saving them. Not researched.
9. **Does the writer-fear-of-loss finding translate to a mobile, offline PWA?** Storage quotas and eviction of IndexedDB on iOS/Android are another researcher's topic but directly affect any Drawer or snapshot promise.
10. **Text-to-speech offline reliability** needs real-device testing (MDN is silent on offline).
11. **Sensitivity and cultural defaults in craft advice.** Nguyen argues "show, don't tell" can disadvantage some writers; Diaz and Nguyen describe default-reader bias in workshops. How should Parchemin's craft questions avoid presenting one tradition as universal? Related to PHILOSOPHY.md's cited teachers (all Western commercial-craft authors).
12. **Dialogue and POV checks:** no source was read in depth; I would not assume they are automatable beyond the writer's own tagging.
13. **Snapshot data-safety bug candidates** noticed in code (cascade delete of snapshots; HTML injection through backup restore) should be passed to whoever owns the app code.
