# 10. The writer's life: wellbeing, isolation, career and AI

Research note for Parchemin. Area: mental health, community, career and money, AI attitudes, and the evidence behind the app's mission claims.

**Method and honesty notes.** I read PHILOSOPHY.md and README.md first. I then used web search and fetches. Midway through, my search budget and then my session limit ran out. This has consequences, stated plainly:

- Every figure below was seen in a fetched page, a search result, or text I extracted myself from a primary PDF. Each source has a quality note in section 7.
- Some topics got thin or no primary coverage: loneliness among writers, critique-group dynamics, social-media comparison, rejection sensitivity, indie-author income surveys, the Writers Guild of America's AI terms, and fan-fiction community reactions. These are marked **UNVERIFIED** or listed as gaps in section 6. Nothing there is written as fact.
- Where I know of a study from background knowledge but did not retrieve it, I say "not retrieved".
- Where a search result summarised a source I did not open, I mark the figure "via search snippet".
- Confidence labels: High = primary source read, or several consistent sources. Medium = secondary report, or one source. Low = snippet only, or weak design.

## 1. Executive summary

1. **Most writers do not earn a living from writing, and the trend is worse.** UK primary-occupation authors had a median self-employed writing income of £7,000 in 2022. That is down 38.2% in real terms from 2018. The share who earn all their income from writing fell from 40% (2006) to 28% (2018) to 19% (2022). US Authors Guild full-time authors had a median book income of $10,000 for 2022. Both surveys are of members or volunteers, so they are skewed, but the direction is consistent. (High.)
2. **Money should be kept out of the promise.** The honest message for hobbyists and early writers is "finish it because it is yours", not "this will pay". The data supports the mission's "even if a book is never published" line, and it argues against any income-flavoured motivational copy.
3. **The "tortured writer" claim is partly true and easy to misuse.** A large Swedish register study found authors specifically had elevated rates of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression, anxiety, substance abuse and suicide. It found creative occupations overall were not at higher risk apart from bipolar disorder. It cannot show that writing causes illness, that illness causes good writing, or anything about hobbyists. Parchemin should not romanticise suffering and should not diagnose. (Medium-High.)
4. **Expressive writing (Pennebaker) has a real but small and contested evidence base.** Frattaroli's 2006 meta-analysis of 146 randomized studies found an average r of .075. Later work found null effects for depression in some meta-analyses, and a 2025 pilot in depressed patients found no benefit over control. Writing about stories is also not the same as writing about trauma. Parchemin must not claim writing is therapy. (High on "small and mixed".)
5. **"Reading fiction makes you more empathic" is weaker than popular belief.** The famous 2013 Science result failed several large replications. A 2018 meta-analysis found a small effect (g about .15) across 14 studies. A 2023 Japanese pair of studies found a correlation but not the causal effect. Evidence for writing building empathy is thinner still; I found none that is strong. The mission wording should be a belief about practice, not a finding. (High on "contested".)
6. **Writers' stance on AI is clearer than the hype suggests.** Across Authors Guild, Society of Authors and Cambridge surveys, writers overwhelmingly reject AI-generated prose and training on their work without consent (about 90-97% on consent and compensation). They are far less hostile to research help, brainstorming and grammar-style checks. Fiction writers use AI less than other professional writers. (High.)
7. **Even the accepted uses are contested.** In the Cambridge novelist survey 97% were extremely opposed to AI writing whole novels and 87% to AI drafting short sections. 43% were extremely negative about AI editing, and writers spoke of losing "friction" and effort. An assistant that only asks questions is the best-supported design. Even that must be opt-in and off by default. (Medium-High.)
8. **The NaNoWriMo episode is a case study in what not to do.** In September 2024 it called categorical condemnation of AI "classist and ableist", and this prompted board resignations and sponsor withdrawals. The nonprofit then shut down in March 2025. Its own leadership blamed a six-year participation decline, so AI was one factor among several. The lesson: do not use disabled or poor writers as a shield for a business position, and ask them. (Medium-High.)
9. **AI can raise individual quality while narrowing what everyone writes.** Doshi and Hauser (2024, Science Advances) found AI ideas made stories rated more creative, especially for less creative writers. The stories also became more similar to each other. Padmakumar and He found a statistically significant diversity reduction with InstructGPT. A writer's own voice is also what Parchemin exists to protect. (Medium-High.)
10. **Evidence on community for fiction writers is thin.** I found good academic evidence that writing groups and retreats help scholars' productivity and belonging. I found no rigorous evidence on hobbyist fiction critique groups. Peer contact is plausible but unproven for this population, so any community feature should be modest and offline-first.

## 2. Problems

Severity scale: High / Medium / Low, for the writer's ability to finish and keep writing.

### 2.1 Money and time: writing does not pay, and a job eats the hours
- **Description.** Most writers write around other work. Writing income is small, unequal and falling. People ask "is it worth it?" and often feel they must justify the hours.
- **Who.** Everyone, but hobbyists feel guilt about time, and professionals feel precarity.
- **Evidence.**
  - UK (ALCS-funded, CREATe, 2022 survey, about 60,000 authors approached, 1,104 gave earnings data): median self-employed writing income of primary-occupation authors £7,000 in 2022. Real terms: £17,608 (2006), £12,455 (2014), £11,329 (2018), £7,000 (2022). Those earning all income from writing: 40% (2006), 28% (2018), 19% (2022). The top 10% earn about 47% of total income. Women, black and mixed-race authors, the very young and the very old earned less. Median household income was £50,000, so many authors rely on other household earners. https://zenodo.org/records/7373314 (report text read from PDF 2022_13_ALCS.pdf). Confidence: High for the stated figures. The sample is a small, self-selected fraction of those invited.
  - US (Authors Guild 2023 Author Income Survey, 5,699 published authors, income year 2022): full-time author median book income $10,000, total author-related income $20,000. Commercial-market full-timers: $15,000 and $25,000. All authors, full and part time: $2,000 book and $5,000 total. By genre, full-time romance had $31,725 book income and literary fiction $5,000. Self-published full-timers: $12,800 book income. Experienced self-published authors: $24,000, up 76% from 2018. Black full-time authors: $2,412 versus $10,985 for white authors. https://authorsguild.org/news/key-takeaways-from-2023-author-income-survey/ Confidence: High on numbers as reported. The survey is of members and organisations' contacts, so it skews toward people already publishing.
  - Queer and disabled fiction writers study (137 professional prose fiction writers): median writing income $1,500 overall and $695.50 for queer and disabled writers. Only 23% wrote full time. https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04575 Confidence: Medium (preliminary, small, 85% white sample).
- **Not verified.** Indie versus trad versus hybrid comparisons beyond the Authors Guild numbers. Other sources I did not retrieve include Written Word Media surveys and ALLi.
- **Severity.** High for professionals. For Parchemin's core audience, it is Medium: the hazard is demoralising writers with money talk, or feeding them hype.

### 2.2 Isolation and the lack of readers
- **Description.** Writing is solitary. Many first-time writers have no one to read their work, no one who says "this is a real thing you are doing", and no sense of what normal progress looks like.
- **Who.** Hobbyists, early writers, and people in places with no local scene. Likely worse for writers who also face stigma or disability, but I did not retrieve direct evidence for this.
- **Evidence.** I did not find a good prevalence study of loneliness among fiction writers. What I found is indirect:
  - Writing groups and retreats among academics increase productivity and belonging. Examples: a mixed-methods evaluation of grassroots initiatives including writing retreats (n=202) found structured, interactive formats with predictable routines and psychological safety fostered belonging (Aman et al., Behavioral Sciences, 2026, doi 10.3390/bs16020226). Cross-institutional writing groups for nurse faculty produced 27 peer-reviewed publications (Bell et al., Nurse Educator, 2026, doi 10.1097/nne.0000000000002211). Both from Europe PMC search results: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=(%22writing%20group%22%20OR%20%22writing%20groups%22%20OR%20%22writing%20retreat%22)%20AND%20(isolation%20OR%20community%20OR%20productivity)&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=8 Confidence: Low-Medium. These are academic writers, descriptive designs, and the outcomes are publications, not fiction finished.
  - Creative writing workshops for transplant recipients were described as therapeutic, and peer connection normalised experiences (BMJ Open, 2026, doi 10.1136/bmjopen-2024-096791, 31 interviews). Search result only. Low-Medium.
  - Cambridge novelist survey comments describe "friction" and effort as valued, which implies writers value the struggle but not that it must be lonely. https://techxplore.com/news/2025-11-novelists-ai.html
- **Severity.** Medium-High as an experience, though I cannot size it.

### 2.3 Depression, anxiety and the "mad genius" story
- **Description.** The cultural story says writers are fragile or must suffer. Real data shows elevated psychiatric risk in authors as a group, with many caveats.
- **Who.** Professional and published authors in the register data. Not hobbyists.
- **Evidence.**
  - Kyaga et al. 2013, Journal of Psychiatric Research, Swedish total-population registers, over 1.1 million people. Overall creative professions were not more likely to have the studied disorders than controls, except bipolar disorder. Authorship specifically showed associations with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression, anxiety, substance abuse and suicide. Relatives of people with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and anorexia nervosa, and siblings of people with autism, were over-represented in creative fields. Abstract via Europe PMC (PMID 23063328, doi 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2012.09.010). The Karolinska press release says authors were "almost 50 per cent more likely" to die by suicide: https://news.ki.se/link-between-creativity-and-mental-illness-confirmed Confidence: Medium-High on the association in Sweden. It is about occupation, not about the act of writing, and it cannot give cause.
  - A Swedish register study of artistic education: students of artistic subjects had higher odds of schizophrenia (OR 1.90), bipolar disorder (OR 1.62) and unipolar depression (OR 1.39), still present after adjusting for education and IQ. Limitations listed by the authors include reverse causation, unmeasured confounders such as substance misuse, and creativity being hard to capture in admin data. (Authors not captured in my fetch.) https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/artistic-creativity-and-risk-for-schizophrenia-bipolar-disorder-and-unipolar-depression-a-swedish-populationbased-casecontrol-study-and-sibpair-analysis/B3FFC439154C19A01F779365AF16B3C7 Confidence: Medium.
  - Reviews: via search snippet, contributors to Kaufman's edited volume *Creativity and Mental Illness* (Cambridge, 2014) argue evidence is strongest for eminent artists and inconsistent in everyday populations. https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358295139_Creativity_and_Mental_Illness (not read in full). Confidence: Low-Medium.
  - The Bookseller 2023 survey: 54% of 108 authors said the publishing process had harmed their mental health (anxiety, stress, depression, low self-esteem). Via search snippet, small and self-selected. Confidence: Low.
- **Misreading to avoid.** Do not tell writers they are likely to be depressed, or that depression helps writing. No evidence supports that for ordinary writers.
- **Severity.** High for some individuals. Parchemin is not a treatment tool and should say so, with a quiet "where to get help" link if it ever touches this.

### 2.4 Rejection, comparison and "not a real writer"
- **Description.** Shame, comparison with others' output, and the feeling that one is not a real writer until published. Rejection letters feel like verdicts.
- **Who.** Anyone who shows work, especially people submitting.
- **Evidence.** **UNVERIFIED.** I did not retrieve studies on rejection sensitivity in writers, impostor feelings or social-media comparison. The Cambridge report and the "80% me, 20% AI" interview study both show writers care about authenticity and process, which is relevant to feeling real (see 2.6). Treat the problem as widely reported anecdotally, with no evidence from me on prevalence.
- **Severity.** Likely High for finishing, unquantified.

### 2.5 Community collapse: critique groups and local scenes
- **Description.** Critique groups often form, then fade or turn unkind. Local scenes depend on a few organisers.
- **Evidence.** **UNVERIFIED** for fiction critique groups. Academic writing-group literature offers success factors (open communication, equitable workload, sustained peer accountability; Bell et al. 2026, above). One notable community failure is NaNoWriMo itself, covered under 2.6.
- **Severity.** Medium.

### 2.6 AI anxiety, and trust in tools
- **Description.** Writers worry AI will take income, copy their voice, and make human work worth less. Tool makers who push AI lose trust fast.
- **Evidence.** See section 4 for full detail. Headlines: 86% of respondents to the Society of Authors survey were concerned about AI mimicking their style and voice, and 86% that it would devalue human creative work. 39% of surveyed British novelists said their income had already been hit by generative AI, and 85% expect future income to be driven down.
- **Severity.** High for trust. A Parchemin feature that touched this wrongly could undo the product's identity.

### 2.7 Procrastination and fear of the blank page
- **Evidence.** Covered by other researchers in this project. I add only that Doshi and Hauser found AI ideas helped less creative writers most, which suggests the need (starting help) is real even if the remedy (generated text) is rejected by most. https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22Doshi%22%20AND%20%22generative%20AI%22%20AND%20%22creativity%22%20AND%20%22short%20stories%22&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=3
- **Severity.** High, and out of scope for this note.

## 3. Evidence check on the mission claims

For each belief in PHILOSOPHY.md: what is supported, what is weak, and suggested wording.

### Belief 1. "Writing changes the world."
- **Supported.** This is a historical and interpretive claim, not a lab result. I retrieved no studies for or against it, and none are needed because PHILOSOPHY.md already frames it as "a belief, not a theorem".
- **Weak or contested.** Causal claims about particular books moving nations are usually hard to demonstrate. I did not review the historiography, so avoid specific attributions in app copy unless a human checks each one. The same applies to quotations used as epigraphs.
- **Honest wording.** "People have always written things down before they built them." Or: "We believe words matter." Avoid "novels start revolutions" and any numbers.

### Belief 2. "Everyone is a storyteller."
- **Supported.** As a statement of permission and dignity, it is fine. There is a broad finding that people construct narratives about their lives. McAdams-style "narrative identity" research links certain narrative patterns (redemptive sequences, agency, communion) with wellbeing. A recent cross-national paper found narrative identity "cannot be fully captured without understanding culture" (Turner et al., 2024, doi 10.1037/pspp0000523), and another found redemptive narration similar across US and UK samples (Mansfield et al., 2025, doi 10.3390/bs15081127). Both via Europe PMC search results: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22narrative%20identity%22%20AND%20%22psychological%20well-being%22%20AND%20Adler&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=4
- **Weak or contested.** That research is correlational and about telling one's own life story, not writing fiction. It does not show that everyone can write a finished novel, and "missing only a system, a quiet room and permission" is a belief, not a finding. I did not retrieve evidence about what proportion of people who start novels finish them.
- **Honest wording.** "You do not need anyone's permission to start." Or: "Most of us tell stories every day." Do not say "everyone has a novel in them" as if it were established, and do not say talent is irrelevant.

### Belief 3a. "Writing helps us see" (attention, craft, self-understanding)
- **Supported (weakly).** Expressive writing research shows small average benefits for some outcomes (Frattaroli 2006: 146 randomized studies, average r of .075, with larger effects in higher-stress samples). https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Experimental-disclosure-and-its-moderators:-a-Frattaroli/4cda801628a1d3fcb126fd0ce1db22d720245017 (via search result). Pennebaker himself reviewed his 1997 paper and the field in 2018 (Perspectives on Psychological Science, doi 10.1177/1745691617707315). Europe PMC record: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22Writing%20about%20emotional%20experiences%20as%20a%20therapeutic%20process%22&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=3
- **Contested.** Rude et al. (2023, Frontiers in Psychology) note three meta-analyses reported small-to-medium benefits, while three others found null effects. One of those, Reinhold et al. 2018, found no benefit on self-reported depression across 39 studies. Rude's own data suggest benefit depends on engagement (essay length), and that standard instructions did not beat control. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1192595/full Reinhold's paper exists (doi 10.1111/cpsp.12224, confirmed via Crossref) but I did not read it. A 2025 pilot RCT in 53 depressed patients found no evidence that condition affected depressive symptoms, and experiences were mixed (Maslej et al., PLOS Mental Health, doi 10.1371/journal.pmen.0000245).
- **Not the same activity.** Pennebaker's paradigm is writing about one's own emotional upheavals for a few days. It is not novel-writing. I found no strong study that fiction writing, craft practice or "finding the exact word" improves attention. That part of Belief 3 is plausible, and I have no evidence for it.
- **Creative writing and wellbeing more broadly.** What I found are low-certainty reviews of arts programmes. One review of arts-inclusive programmes for children rated certainty low or very low (Frontiers in Health Services, 2026, doi 10.3389/frhs.2026.1827784). One quasi-experimental study (n=120) of creative writing in chronic pain reported reduced anxiety and depression but stable pain (Prassas et al., Cureus, 2026, doi 10.7759/cureus.112460). A scoping review of prison creative-writing programmes found most studies lacked rigorous evaluation (Duursma et al., 2025, doi 10.1177/0306624x251346511). All via Europe PMC search results: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22creative%20writing%22%20AND%20(randomized%20OR%20randomised%20OR%20intervention)%20AND%20(depression%20OR%20anxiety%20OR%20wellbeing)&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=8
- **Honest wording.** "Many people find that writing things down helps them think. The research on this is mixed, so we won't promise it." Or: "Looking closely enough to find the right word is a way of paying attention." (Craft claim, framed as craft.) Do not write "writing heals", "journaling cures anxiety", or "proven to improve wellbeing".

### Belief 3b. "Writing builds empathy"
- **Supported.** Some correlational evidence that fiction exposure goes with better social cognition. Dodell-Feder and Tamir 2018 (J Exp Psychol General, doi 10.1037/xge0000395): 53 effect sizes from 14 studies, fiction reading leads to a small, significant improvement in social-cognitive performance (g of .15-.16), with no evidence of publication bias. Source: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22fiction%20reading%20has%20a%20small%20positive%20impact%20on%20social%20cognition%22&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=2 Mumper and Gerrig 2017 also meta-analysed leisure reading and social cognition (doi 10.1037/aca0000089, existence confirmed via Crossref; results not retrieved).
- **Contested.** Kidd and Castano's 2013 Science finding that a short passage of literary fiction improves theory of mind has been challenged. Two large replications failed (Panero et al. 2016; Samur et al. 2017), and Kidd and Castano's own reanalysis of Panero's data under their preferred exclusions still did not reproduce their pattern. Via search results, which link to a Collabra replication, a PubMed record and a Cognition and Emotion paper: https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/4/1/7/112955/The-Effect-of-Reading-a-Short-Passage-of-Literary and https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02699931.2017.1279591 (neither was opened by me; my summary is from the search result text). A 2023 Japanese pair of studies found a correlation (N=338) but "failed to replicate the causal relationship" (N=304) (Takahashi et al., PLOS ONE, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0287542). A 2022 review (Eekhof et al., Psychonomic Bulletin and Review, doi 10.3758/s13423-022-02079-z) calls for caution after failed replications. The sources here are Europe PMC search results.
- **Writing, not reading.** The empathy literature is about reading. I found no strong study that writing a character unlike yourself builds empathy. The claim in PHILOSOPHY.md ("writing a character who is not you means understanding someone who is not you") is a reasonable description of craft, and I have no experimental support for it as a general effect.
- **Honest wording.** "Writing someone unlike you asks you to imagine how they see things. That is a skill worth practising." Do not say "reading and writing make you more empathic" as a finding. Do not cite Kidd and Castano as if settled.

### Belief 4 (added for completeness). "Writing is learnable / finishing is a skill."
- **Evidence.** I did not retrieve evidence on habit formation or on finishing. See other notes.
- **Wording.** Present the 15-chapter novella and 30-chapter novel pacing as a plan Parchemin offers, not as a statistic.

### Beliefs worth a flat disclaimer
Parchemin is a writing app. It is not mental-health care. A one-line "if writing brings up something heavy, talking to someone helps" in a quiet settings page costs little and avoids overclaiming.

The May 2023 row and the 47% grammar figure come from a search result summary, not from opening the page.

**Cautions about the data.**
- All of these are self-selected surveys of guild members or similar groups. They show what organised, published writers think, not the full range of hobbyists and fan writers.
- The Authors Guild survey figures on use vary across rounds (23% in May 2023, 13% in the later round, depending on how the question was asked), so do not compare them directly.
- Hobbyists, non-native speakers, and people with disabilities may feel differently about assistance. The one study I read directly found queer and disabled writers more negative about AI's industry impact, not less. It found limited explicit discussion linking disability to wanting AI help, so that area is under-researched. Do not assume.
- Readers may not detect AI help. In an interview study of 19 professional writers, 30 readers "could not distinguish AI-assisted work... from writers' solo-written work" (Hwang et al. 2024). This does not mean writers wanted it. Writers there located authenticity in "the process and experience of constructing creators' authentic selves". https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.13032
- Ippolito et al. (2022, 13 published authors using Wordcraft) found that language-generation tools "struggle to preserve style and authorial voice", with promise in brainstorming and world-building. Via search result: http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05030v1

### 4.2 What writers accept versus reject

**Most accepted (still contested):**
- General research and fact lookup. Note that 9 in 10 working writers worry about hallucinations.
- Brainstorming, often as a question-asker rather than an answer-giver.
- Grammar and spelling, by this evidence the most common use among Authors Guild AI-using respondents. This is a tool category writers used long before generative AI.
- Accessibility tools. The evidence I read is thin and I would not treat "accessibility" as a settled reason for AI prose generation. See the NaNoWriMo case below.

**Most rejected:**
- AI-generated prose, whole or in part (97% extremely opposed to whole novels, 87% to short sections in Cambridge data).
- Training on writers' work without consent and payment (90-97% across surveys).
- AI standing in for editors or critics (43% extremely negative about AI editing; queer and disabled writers rejected AI manuscript evaluation).
- Unlabelled AI content (91-97% want disclosure).

**Effects on writing itself.**
- Doshi and Hauser (2024): access to AI ideas made stories more creative, better written and more enjoyable, "especially among less creative writers", but the AI-assisted stories were more similar to one another. https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22Doshi%22%20AND%20%22generative%20AI%22%20AND%20%22creativity%22%20AND%20%22short%20stories%22&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=3 (via Europe PMC record).
- Padmakumar and He (arXiv, updated 2024): writing with InstructGPT reduced diversity significantly; the model's contributions, not the humans' own text, were less varied. http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05196v3
- Jakesch et al. (2023, 1,506 participants): an opinionated writing assistant shifted both what people wrote and what they later said they believed. http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00560v1 These were argumentative or social-media topics, not fiction, but it shows assistants can steer a writer silently.

### 4.3 The NaNoWriMo episode (September 2024 to March 2025)
- In late August or early September 2024 NaNoWriMo published a statement saying it neither supports nor condemns any approach "including the use of AI". It said that categorical condemnation of AI "has classist and ableist undertones", and tied the question to privilege and the cost of human feedback. https://www.404media.co/nanowrimo-ai-policy-classist-ableist/
- Resignations followed: Daniel Jose Older left the Writers Board, and Maureen Johnson left the Young Writers Program board. Others, including Silvia Moreno-Garcia and Erin Morgenstern, distanced themselves. Several sponsors withdrew (Wikipedia). NaNoWriMo amended its position more than once and ended at "neutrality" (Publishers Weekly). https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/industry-news/publisher-news/article/97466-nanowrimo-nonprofit-shutters.html
- Disabled writers objected to being used as a justification (via search-result summaries only; I did not read the primary posts).
- The nonprofit announced it would close on March 31, 2025. Its interim director cited "a six-year downward trend in participation" and said it was "not a salacious tale of scandal" (PW). Wikipedia instead reports "financial struggles and community vitriol", and notes other scandals including child-safety moderation failures. Reading both, AI was one injury among several and not the sole cause. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NaNoWriMo (secondary).
- **Lessons.** (1) A creative-writing community will punish neutrality that sounds like permission for generated prose. (2) Accessibility and cost are real concerns that deserve direct answers, not slogans. (3) Do not claim to speak for disabled or poor writers. Ask them, or serve them with non-generative tools.

### 4.4 Lawsuits, at a high level (secondary sources, check before citing)
- Authors Guild v. OpenAI was filed September 2023 on behalf of 17 authors, including George R. R. Martin, and was pending when the article I read was written. Other author suits against OpenAI and Meta (Tremblay, Awad, Silverman, Kadrey and others) were consolidated, with most claims dismissed in early 2024 except the training-data infringement claim. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_and_copyright
- Bartz v. Anthropic: the court held that training on legally purchased books was fair use, but that acquiring and keeping about seven million pirated library copies was not. The case settled for $1.5 billion, about $3,000 per book. Wikipedia reports the settlement as September 2025 in one article and August 2025 in another, with final approval in July 2026. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartz_v._Anthropic Confidence: Medium (secondary, details differ across pages).
- **Why it matters for Parchemin.** It makes no-training and no-text-harvesting a design and trust feature. It also means any external model the app calls must have a contractual no-training guarantee, or the app should not send the text at all.

### 4.6 Recommended policy for any future opt-in assistant in Parchemin

**Principles (extending PHILOSOPHY.md):**
1. **Off by default, per device, per story.** No assistant UI is visible until the writer turns it on in Settings. A one-time plain-language screen says what it does and what leaves the device.
2. **Questions, not sentences.** The assistant may ask questions about your text, point to places where a rule from the craft guide seems unmet ("What does she want in this scene?"), and answer craft or research questions. It never outputs story prose, rewrites, "improvements", continuations or alternative phrasings of the writer's sentences.
3. **Nothing leaves the device without a clear, per-use choice.** Preferred: no network call at all (on-device model, or only the app's own rule-based checks). If a hosted model is used: bring your own key, send only the selection the writer chose, show exactly what will be sent before sending, and use a provider with a written no-training, minimal-retention term. Never send the whole library. Never send by background process.
4. **No training, no analytics, no logging of text.** Prompts and replies are not stored by Parchemin beyond the session unless the writer saves a note, and they are never included in telemetry. Parchemin has no telemetry in this project, so keep it that way.
5. **Label everything.** Anything the assistant produced is visibly marked and kept apart from the manuscript. Nothing is ever inserted into the text automatically, and copying it is a deliberate act, so there is no way to accidentally "write with AI".
6. **Respect voice.** Do not offer style imitation, "make it sound like X", or voice-matching. 86% of SoA respondents fear it, and Ippolito et al. found tools struggle to keep voice anyway.
7. **Accessibility without generation.** Prefer non-generative accommodations first: dictation (browser speech-to-text is often cloud-based, so check and label), read-aloud (browser text-to-speech works offline on most systems), adjustable fonts and spacing, a dyslexia-friendly font option, and a spelling check. Put these in Settings regardless of whether any assistant exists. If a disabled writer asks for more, ask them and treat it as a design conversation, not a loophole.
8. **Disclosure support, not disclosure policing.** Because 91-97% want readers told, export could include an optional "made without generative AI" or "used AI for X" line, which the writer writes. Never auto-detect.
9. **A real "no".** Turning it off removes all UI and deletes any stored prompts. The writer should never see "enable AI for a better experience" banners.
10. **Design for homogeneity.** The two diversity studies suggest suggestions pull writers toward the average. Keep the assistant's questions open-ended and varied, and avoid showing "example answers" or "sample sentences".

**Decision test for each assistant feature (from PHILOSOPHY.md):** does it help a writer finish their own story, keep them in control of words and data, stay honest, teach by use, keep the room calm and work offline? Reject if any answer is no. Cloud calls are inherently in tension with the last test, so they stay opt-in and clearly labelled as exceptions.

**Ranking of assistant behaviours:**
1. Allowed: craft-question prompts, rule checks, research questions. (Closest to the accepted-use data.)
2. Allowed with care: "read-back" summaries, labelled, that tell the writer what the text seems to say so they can check it against intent. Risk: the summary becomes an editor. Medium confidence. Present only on request.
3. Not allowed: any generated prose, rewriting, tone matching, continuation, "fix my prose", manuscript scoring, or comparison to bestsellers.

### Rejected ideas (explicitly)
- **Public leaderboards, word-count rankings, "writing buddies" feeds, or any social comparison.** Comparison is a named harm, and it breaks "no leaderboards".
- **Streak-loss warnings, guilt notifications, "you have not written in 3 days" emails.** Contradicts PHILOSOPHY.md and the question 3 test.
- **Mood tracking or "writing improves your mood" scores.** The evidence is mixed, it implies medical claims, and it means collecting sensitive data.
- **Telling writers they are at risk of depression, or "creative people are more sensitive".** Overclaims and stigmatises. See 2.3.
- **Any generated prose, "fix my prose", continue-the-story, style imitation or AI manuscript scoring.** Rejected by 87-97% of surveyed novelists, and forbidden by PHILOSOPHY.md.
- **Selling income hype** ("write a bestseller in 30 days", "turn your story into income"). The income data contradicts it.
- **Default-on AI features, AI "nudges", or upsells.** Violates opt-in and calm.
- **Analytics on what people write, or training on their text.** Violates PHILOSOPHY.md and writers' top stated demand (consent).
- **Auto-detecting AI use or "plagiarism" scoring** of writers' own work. It would be unreliable and hostile.
- **Using disability or poverty as the argument for an AI feature** without consulting disabled writers (the NaNoWriMo mistake).

## 6. Open questions and gaps

1. **Hobbyist and fan-writer voices.** Almost all AI surveys sample published, guild-affiliated professionals. How do unpublished writers, fan writers and people writing for themselves feel about an opt-in question-asking assistant? The 2024 Wattpad figures I saw are unverified. Parchemin's real users may differ.
2. **Disability and accessibility.** I found no good study of what disabled writers actually want from AI tools, only that queer and disabled professionals are more pessimistic. This needs direct research, ideally by asking disabled writers, before accessibility is used to justify any AI feature.
3. **Does writing a finished story help wellbeing?** There is evidence for expressive writing (small, contested), and only low-certainty evidence for creative writing programmes. Nothing I found tests "finishing a novella". The app should not claim it.
4. **Does a rule-based craft guide help people finish?** This is the core product bet and I found no evidence either way in this area. Other notes may cover it.
5. **Loneliness among writers, critique-group survival, retreats, local scenes, rejection sensitivity, social-media comparison.** UNVERIFIED, no primary data retrieved. A follow-up researcher should search for these with working tools.
6. **Indie versus trad versus hybrid income.** Only Authors Guild and ALCS figures were retrieved. Written Word Media, ALLi and Reedsy data were not.
7. **Writers Guild of America and screenwriting AI terms.** Not researched, and I would not include them without a source.
8. **Legal state of AI training.** The Bartz ruling splits "purchased and trained" from "pirated". Other cases are still moving, and the Wikipedia sources conflict on dates. Re-check before saying anything legal in the app.
9. **On-device models.** Could a small local model run questions-only features in the browser with no data leaving the device? That would satisfy offline and no-harvest, but I did not research feasibility, size or quality.
10. **Representativeness of the income surveys.** The UK earnings data came from about 1,100 respondents out of roughly 60,000 invited, and the US data from 5,699 authors recruited through 36 organisations. Both are self-selected, so medians may be biased either way. They are consistent in direction with each other.
