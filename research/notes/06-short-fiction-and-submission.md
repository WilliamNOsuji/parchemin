# 06. Short fiction, flash, novellas and submission

Researcher 6 of 12. Area: short stories, flash, novellas, markets, submission process, tracking, formatting, rejection, serialized publication.
Date of research: 2026-10-03.

Method note. About 45 fetches and searches were done. The shared web-search budget ran out partway through (200/200), so some planned searches did not run: simultaneous-submission etiquette, serial fiction on Substack/Royal Road, reading as a writer, short-story-to-novel evidence. Those are listed as gaps in section 5. Many pages were summarised by the fetch tool's small model, not read in full, so quotes are short and paraphrase is preferred. Confidence labels: HIGH = primary source or several independent sources agree; MED = one reasonable source or first-hand anecdote; LOW = second-hand, paywalled or unverified.

---

## 1. Executive summary

1. The hard part of short fiction is not length. It is fit. Editors reject for slow openings, no conflict, "too much" for the size, and endings that explain or fail. "Is this idea the right size for this form?" is the question writers most often cannot answer alone.
2. Endings are repeatedly named as the hardest part. The craft advice converges: end on an image or action, not on a character stating the meaning. Echo an earlier object or phrase (Tommy Dean, Jennifer Murvin, Susan DeFreitas).
3. Submission is a numbers game with very low odds. Literary-magazine fiction acceptance is commonly cited as about 1 to 1.5 percent. The Missouri Review says 0.63 percent. One writer sent 50 stories in 2020 before her first acceptance. Writers who persist track everything, and they treat rejection as a routine, not a verdict.
4. Tracking is solved poorly. Most writers use a spreadsheet. Dedicated tools (Duotrope at $50 a year, the free Submission Grinder, Chill Subs, Story Tracker) exist, but writers complain about cost, fragmentation (Submittable, email, Duosuma and others all at once), clunky data entry, and accounts. Nobody offers a private, offline, account-free tracker that also knows your manuscripts.
5. Manuscript formatting (Shunn) is a real, small, recurring chore. Word processors default to the wrong settings (indents, headers, page numbers). Some editors reject over bad formatting. Parchemin already holds the text and the title, so generating a correct manuscript is a natural fit.
6. Market-data tools double as anxiety machines. One Grinder user admits checking it many times a day and reading tea leaves in response times. A calm tool should show a tracker without a refresh-and-fret loop.
7. Submission economics are shifting. Fees are common among prestigious magazines (56 percent of the top 100 charge, per a Chill Subs figure cited in Lit Hub). Submittable takes a cut. Writers resent paying to submit. A tracker that records cost per submission is useful, and the app should never push paid markets.
8. Editors now explicitly ban AI-written work (Clarkesworld; its submissions surged about 35 times in 2023, per Wikipedia). Parchemin's "never ghostwrite" stance matches the short-fiction community's own values and can be said plainly.
9. Word-count categories are standard enough to build on: flash up to about 1,500; short story under 7,500; novelette 7,500 to 17,500; novella 17,500 to 40,000; novel 40,000 and up (SFWA/Nebula rules). Parchemin already says a chapter is about the size of a short story, so size awareness is on-brand.
10. Serialization is volatile. Kindle Vella launched in 2021 and closed in February 2025. Writers who serialized had no export path of their own. Parchemin's local-first, open-export design is the opposite of that risk.

## 2. Problems

Each entry: description, who it affects, evidence, confidence, severity (low, medium, high for the writer's ability to finish and share work).

### P1. Knowing the story's size (too big, too small, wrong form)
- Description: Writers cannot tell whether an idea is a flash, a short story, a novella or a novel. Stories balloon past word limits. Others are an "anecdote" with no turn. Editors call this "Not a short story" and "Too much".
- Affects: new short-story writers, novelists trying short form, anyone writing to a market limit.
- Evidence:
  - Susan DeFreitas (LitReactor) lists "Not a Short Story" and "Too Much" among the top reasons she rejects. URL: https://litreactor.com/columns/the-top-10-reasons-i-may-have-rejected-your-short-story/ (HIGH that the editor says it; one editor's view).
  - Kathleen Jennings says staying on length "does not come naturally" to her and lists techniques: closed-loop shapes, a narrow viewpoint, a short time span, endings anchored early. URL: https://tanaudel.wordpress.com/2023/08/17/some-ways-to-keep-a-story-short-a-very-long-blog-post/ (MED, working writer's first-hand account).
  - Devon Halliday: a short-story idea is "answerable"; a novel idea holds a tension that persists. URL: https://devonhalliday.substack.com/p/idea-size (MED, one blog).
  - AutoCrit: short stories have one thread, a small cast and a compressed time frame; novellas restrict scope and make each scene do double duty. URL: https://www.autocrit.com/editing/library/does-size-matter-writing-fiction-of-different-lengths/ (MED, vendor blog, but consistent with other sources).
  - Kyell Gold: a novel "starts out far away from what the story is about"; the move to novels is mostly about sustained scope. URL: https://kyellgold.substack.com/p/writing-advice-from-short-to-novels (MED).
- Severity: High for finishing. A story with the wrong size stalls or gets cut blindly.

### P2. Endings
- Description: Endings must be in character and beyond it. Writers either summarise the meaning, rely on a twist or withheld fact, or just stop.
- Affects: all short-fiction and flash writers.
- Evidence:
  - Tommy Dean on flash endings: avoid ending on exposition or thought, avoid "pat, one-sided, summarizing" closes, avoid withheld-information tricks; reuse earlier images in a new way. URL: https://tommydean.substack.com/p/a-few-thoughts-on-flash-endings (MED-HIGH, working flash teacher).
  - Jennifer Murvin (CRAFT) names four ending moves: recollection and meditation, suspended symbolic action, rhyming action, surprise memory. Each needs both thought and gesture. URL: https://www.craftliterary.com/2022/11/08/crafting-endings-in-short-fiction/ (MED-HIGH).
  - DeFreitas: "Endings are often harder than beginnings" (paraphrase of a quote) in the same LitReactor list. Aigner Loren Wilson (The Writer) lists "disconnected endings" that fail to follow through on earlier promises. URL: https://www.writermag.com/get-published/the-publishing-industry/slush-pile-mistakes/ (MED).
- Severity: High. Writers abandon or ruin otherwise sound drafts here.

### P3. Openings and the two-page window
- Description: Slush readers decide fast. Slow starts, front-loaded exposition, confusion about who, where and what, and no conflict by page six cause rejection.
- Affects: submitting writers.
- Evidence: DeFreitas says editors "generally only got two pages" to be won over (LitReactor URL above; HIGH on editor claim, anecdotal on how typical). The Writer (Wilson) lists long-winded openings first. Anthony Varallo: a flash piece should already be in motion by the first punctuation mark (paraphrase). URL: https://www.writermag.com/improve-your-writing/fiction/how-to-write-flash-fiction/ (MED).
- Severity: Medium-High.

### P4. Compression, especially flash
- Description: Compressing is not shrinking. A cut-down short story reads rushed; flash has to be built at scale from the start. Writers fail by cutting evenly rather than by choosing what carries meaning.
- Affects: flash writers, anyone cutting to a limit.
- Evidence:
  - Flash Fiction Magazine: compression "isn't subtraction. It's intensification" (quote under 25 words, paraphrase of context). URL: https://flashfictionmagazine.com/the-art-of-compression/ (MED).
  - Writer's Atelier exercise: tie one physical detail to one emotion; mark each sentence as potent or flat and revise until all are strong. URL: https://writersatelier.com/on-compression-a-mini-flash-fiction-workshop/ (LOW-MED, a single exercise).
  - The Writer's expert panel (Michelle Ross, Sherrie Flick, Phong Nguyen): flabby sentences, gimmicks, vagueness confused with ambiguity. URL as above (MED).
  - Cutting advice is generic and everywhere (remove adverbs, cut subplots). Example: https://thewritepractice.com/how-to-shorten-a-story/ (LOW, listicle). The real need is deciding what to cut, and seeing the word count against a target while doing it.
- Severity: Medium.

### P5. Manuscript formatting (Shunn) is fiddly
- Description: Editors expect "standard manuscript format". The usual version is Shunn's: 1 inch margins, 12 point Times New Roman or Courier, double spacing, header on later pages with surname, a short title keyword and page number, word count on page one rounded to the nearest hundred, a title about halfway down the first page. Word processors default to something else. Writers have to find the right menus in Word or Google Docs, set first-line indents (not tabs), and build headers.
- Affects: anyone submitting to magazines, plus contest entrants. Beginners are hit hardest.
- Evidence:
  - Shunn's own guide: set one-inch margins, black type only, 12 points; Times New Roman for the modern version, Courier for "Shunn Classic". "Editors will more eagerly read a cleanly formatted manuscript than a cluttered one" (summary of a quoted line). URL: https://www.shunn.net/format/story/ (HIGH on what is on the page; the fetch tool returned only the first of several pages, so scene-break and end-marker rules are not independently confirmed here).
  - Redlagoe blog: paragraph indents are "one reason some publishers/editors auto-reject stories"; settings differ between Word and Google Docs; headers take several menu steps. URL: https://redlagoe.com/2021/04/29/formatting-your-manuscript-a-brief-guide-to-shunn/ (MED, first-hand, one writer).
  - Wikipedia explains the history: monospaced type once made word counts predictable, and page headers let a dropped, unstapled manuscript be reassembled. "Individual publishers' standards will take precedence." URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_manuscript_format (MED-HIGH).
  - Clarkesworld requires "standard manuscript format" as .RTF or .DOCX and links to Shunn. URL: https://clarkesworldmagazine.com/submissions/ (HIGH, primary).
  - Not confirmed from the pages I could read, and labelled unverified: Shunn modern format uses a centred "#" for scene breaks, italics instead of underlining, and "The End" at the finish. These are widely known but I did not see them on a fetched page.
- Severity: Low-Medium per submission, but it is a barrier and recurs with every story.

### P6. Submission tracking is fragmented and clunky
- Description: Writers must remember which story went where, when, under which policy, at what cost, and what the response was. Magazines use Submittable, Duosuma, Oleada, email, web forms and even post. A story can be pending in six places. Mistakes mean sending the wrong story, resubmitting a rejected story, or forgetting to withdraw a simultaneous submission.
- Affects: any active submitter, worst for prolific writers and poets (hundreds of titles).
- Evidence:
  - Kendall Dunkelberg built her own LibreOffice Base tracker because online forms were "clunky" for hundreds of poems, and says tracking across Submittable, Duosuma, Oleada, email and post "gets quite complicated". URL: https://kendalldunkelberg.com/2022/02/21/writers-how-to-find-and-track-submissions/ (MED-HIGH, first-hand).
  - Katie Lewis: tracking prevents resubmitting the same work to the same editors, helps with simultaneous-submission rules and contest-fee budgets; "for your own sanity". URL: https://katielewiscreative.com/blog/track-your-writing-submissions (MED).
  - April Davila's free spreadsheet has ten columns: status, rank, story, date, journal, submission window, cost, circulation, max word count, prize or payment. URL: https://aprildavila.com/updated-submission-spreadsheet-free/ (MED, shows what a writer actually needs).
  - A 2022-era roundup says a spreadsheet is the most common method and not all magazines use Submittable. URL: https://litmaglab.substack.com/p/the-most-common-ways-writers-and (LOW-MED, a newsletter, not a survey; no percentages).
  - Mackenzie Kincaid compared Trello, the Grinder, Duotrope, spreadsheets and the Story Tracker app: Trello needs setup, spreadsheets need skill, Story Tracker apps cost $7.99 to $29.99 and do not sync. URL: https://mackenziekincaid.com/writing/5-ways-to-track-your-short-story-submissions/ (MED).
- Severity: Medium. Poor tracking costs time, small money and credibility, and feeds anxiety.

### P7. Rejection: volume, odds and emotional cost
- Description: Odds are low and responses are mostly form rejections. Writers lose heart, especially in the first years. The practical coping tools are routines and tracking.
- Affects: nearly every writer who submits.
- Evidence:
  - Helen De Cruz submitted 50 stories in 2020 before her first acceptance in November 2020; she cites "1 to 3 percent" acceptance, three kinds of rejection, and says she lost motivation for two years. URL: https://helensreflectionsblog.wordpress.com/2021/02/12/four-years-of-learning-the-craft/ (MED, first-hand).
  - The Missouri Review: essays by Kyle Burton and Michael Nye; Burton cites a .63 percent acceptance rate. URL: https://missourireview.com/tag/rejections/ (MED-HIGH on editorial voice).
  - ARDOR editor Joe Hessert: form rejections do not mean a poor story; The Iowa Review took 4 or 5 of about 7,000 unsolicited fiction manuscripts in four months (his recollection, 2005 to 2006); he recommends simultaneous submission to soften rejection. URL: http://www.ardorlitmag.com/blog/from-the-editor-how-to-interpret-rejection (MED-HIGH, first-hand editor).
  - Women Who Submit: cites Duotrope data (New Yorker 0.14 percent of 1,447 unsolicited; Split Lip 0.11 percent of 938; Dryland about 1 to 1.5 percent for fiction) and the 2015 Atlantic claim that the average published story is rejected about 20 times. URL: https://womenwhosubmitlit.org/2021/06/16/submissions-the-harsh-reality-and-how-to-improve-your-odds/ (MED; the "20 times" is second-hand and I did not read the Atlantic piece, so treat as LOW).
  - Submitit: a five-minute "pity party", then log it, delete the email, and do a small reframing act; "100 rejections a year" as a goal. URL: https://www.submititnow.com/post/zen-and-the-art-of-getting-rejected (MED, one writer's ritual; "reject-quota" gamification is a double-edged idea, see ideas).
- Severity: High for persistence. Writers quit here.

### P8. Market-data anxiety loop
- Description: Response-time statistics are useful, but writers over-check them and read meaning into timing.
- Affects: submitters with pending stories.
- Evidence: Sara Codair, an admirer of the Grinder, writes that she checks it "multiple times a day" and studies how long markets take to accept versus reject. URL: https://saracodair.com/2016/05/a-review-of-the-grinder-and-its-uses/ (MED, first-hand but 2016).
- Severity: Low-Medium, but directly relevant to the "calm" promise.

### P9. Cost of submitting (fees and platform cuts)
- Description: Many magazines now charge about $3 to submit, and contests $15 to $25. Writers who earn little pay to be rejected.
- Affects: all submitters, especially international writers.
- Evidence:
  - Lit Hub: Chill Subs data says 13 percent of tracked magazines charge fees, 24 percent of the top 1,000 and 56 percent of the top 100; a Karan Kapoor quote about costs in India. URL: https://lithub.com/the-rise-of-the-submission-industrial-complex/ (MED-HIGH, but the figures are second-hand through that article).
  - Melissa Olson Petrie: Submittable charges magazines a yearly fee plus per-submission fees; of a $3 fee the magazine keeps about $1.86. URL: https://melissaolsonpetrie.com/2020/12/22/submittable-follow-the-money-in-literary-magazine-publishing/ (MED; the article had a correction on its totals, so use only the per-submission mechanics).
  - Katie Lewis: contest fees $15 to $25 each (above).
  - Editors defend fees as needed revenue (The Sun cited at about $18,000 a year for Submittable, per Lit Hub). Paywalled editorials were not readable (Lit Mag News, Beyond Craft): LOW.
- Severity: Medium.

### P10. Rules vary and are easy to break
- Description: Each market has word limits, simultaneous-submission rules, reprint rules, reading windows, AI policies and rules for waiting after a rejection.
- Evidence: Clarkesworld: 1,000 to 22,000 words with "no exceptions", no simultaneous submissions, no rejected-story resubmissions, seven-day wait between stories, no AI-assisted work, 14 cents per word. URL: https://clarkesworldmagazine.com/submissions/ (HIGH, primary, but one magazine). Katie Lewis (above) notes confusion about simultaneous-submission policies.
- Severity: Medium.

### P11. Cover letters
- Description: Writers over-think them. Editors say keep it short.
- Evidence: Neil Clarke (Clarkesworld) reads cover letters last, says repeating title, word count and email is pointless, never list more than three credits, and "Thank you for considering my story" is enough if nothing else applies. URL: https://neil-clarke.com/short-story-cover-letters/ (HIGH, primary editor). Alex Shvartsman: "Do not. Summarize. Your Story." URL: https://alexshvartsman.com/2016/05/09/how-to-write-a-proper-short-story-cover-letter/ (MED-HIGH).
- Severity: Low.

### P12. Novellas have no natural home
- Description: Novella-length work (17,500 to 40,000 words) is hard to place: traditional houses see it as costly to produce and low priced; most literary magazines cap well below it; speculative fiction has more outlets.
- Evidence: Kindlepreneur summary (word ranges, marketing difficulty, "avoid labelling under 40,000 words as a novel"). URL: https://kindlepreneur.com/how-long-short-story/ (LOW-MED, self-publishing blog). Word ranges themselves: Nebula rules via search result (SFWA) https://nebulas.sfwa.org/about-the-nebulas/nebula-rules/ (the page was in search results but I did not open it; HIGH on the ranges because several sources agree).
- Severity: Medium for novella writers. Note Parchemin's finish line already treats a novella as 15 chapters.

### P13. Serial publication is platform-dependent
- Description: Writers who serialize on Wattpad, Royal Road, Kindle Vella or Substack depend on someone else's platform and schedule.
- Evidence:
  - Kindle Vella launched 2021 and was discontinued in February 2025; Android Central criticised its token model and Amazon ties. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kindle_Vella (MED, encyclopedia summary).
  - Wikipedia's web-serial article: "very rare to make money" from web fiction; platforms include Wattpad, Royal Road and ScribbleHub. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_serial (LOW-MED).
  - Wattpad: in a 2024 poll cited on Wikipedia, 92 percent of authors said humans should stay involved in writing. URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wattpad (LOW-MED; details of that poll unverified).
  - Missing: first-hand accounts of serial-writing burnout, release schedules and Substack practice. I could not search for these (budget). Do not draw conclusions beyond platform risk.
- Severity: Medium (unknown depth).

### P14. AI floods and trust
- Description: Short-fiction editors face AI-written spam. Some closed submissions.
- Evidence: Clarkesworld halted submissions on 20 February 2023 after "35 times as many" fake submissions (Wikipedia). URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarkesworld_Magazine (MED). Clarkesworld's current guideline bans AI-written or assisted work (HIGH, primary URL above).
- Severity: Medium. It means writers will be asked, more and more, to be able to show their own process.

Gap summary: (a) no tool combines the manuscript, a manuscript-format export and a private submission log in one offline place; (b) the existing cloud trackers require accounts; (c) tracking does not cover non-platform submissions unless entered by hand; (d) calm, non-anxious design is rare.

## 4. Ideas for Parchemin

Philosophy tests used: the six questions from PHILOSOPHY.md (1 start/continue/finish a story that is theirs; 2 writer controls words, data, structure; 3 honest, no manufactured urgency or guilt; 4 teaches by being used; 5 calm; 6 offline, local, no account). Sizes: S = days, M = 1 to 3 weeks, L = more.

### I1. Manuscript-format export (Shunn modern) as .docx and PDF
- What: "Export for submission". Writer enters or confirms byline, real name and contact, and the app generates a 12 point, double-spaced, one-inch-margin manuscript with the first-page block, title, word count rounded to the nearest hundred, and a running header (surname / title keyword / page). Offer Times New Roman and a "Courier" option. Scene breaks from the editor map to the conventional marker. Provide a "This market wants something else" note, since editors' house styles take precedence.
- Fit: Q1 yes (helps a finished story go out), Q2 yes, Q3 yes, Q5 yes, Q6 yes. Not in conflict with "no mandatory structure": it is an export, never a requirement.
- Offline: fully feasible with client-side docx generation (for example the `docx` npm library) and print-to-PDF. Pagination for headers is easier in .docx than in HTML print.
- Evidence of need: P5 (redlagoe; Clarkesworld asks for .docx or .rtf).
- Size: M. Risk: header with page numbers and "word count" accuracy must be tested against Word and Google Docs. Verify the exact scene-break and end-marker conventions on shunn.net before building (not confirmed here).
- Recommendation: build. This is the single clearest, evidence-backed feature in this area.

### I2. Local submission log ("Submissions" tab per story)
- What: For each story, a private list of submissions: market name, date, method (Submittable, email, other), simultaneous-submission flag, cost, status (pending, accepted, rejected, withdrawn, never heard), response date, notes (the rejection text pasted in, if the writer wants). A single overview groups stories by status. Include the exact manuscript snapshot sent (a link to a Parchemin snapshot, which already exists) so the writer knows which draft each market saw.
- Fit: Q1 yes (finishing means sending), Q2 yes, Q3 yes if designed without nagging, Q5 yes, Q6 yes. Aligns with the "no data harvesting" rule: no stats are uploaded.
- Offline: trivially yes (IndexedDB via Dexie; also included in the JSON backup).
- Evidence of need: P6 (Dunkelberg, Lewis, Davila, Kincaid). The key differentiator: the log lives next to the manuscript, and it works with any submission method, including email and post.
- Size: M.
- Include CSV import and export (Chill Subs offers both; the Grinder FAQ mentions neither). Lets writers leave Duotrope or the Grinder without retyping (a clean way out, per the philosophy).
- Caution: no streaks, no "rejection quota" counters unless the writer opts in. See I9.

### I3. Gentle "waiting" view, not a notification system
- What: Pending submissions show how long they have been out, with a plain line such as "Out 62 days. Many magazines take longer." No push notifications, no red alerts. The writer can type a personal "check back on" date, and nothing else.
- Fit: Q3 and Q5, deliberately avoids the refresh-and-fret loop (P8). Q6 yes.
- Offline: yes. Size: S.
- Do not import response-time statistics from the Grinder or Duotrope by default; that would need network access and accounts. A bundled static "typical response times" table would go stale and be unverified, so reject unless sourced.

### I4. Word-limit and form-size targets
- What: Optional per-story "target length" with presets (flash up to about 1,500; short story under 7,500; novelette; novella 17,500 to 40,000; or a custom market limit like 5,000). Show the live count against the target as a quiet bar in the editor footer, with copy like "You are 400 words over Clarkesworld's maximum" only if the writer set that number. Pair with a "cut list": a saved list of passages marked "might go" that do not leave the document until the writer decides.
- Fit: Q1, Q2 (writer picks the number), Q3 (no pressure, no shaming), Q4 teaches the shape of each form. The finish line already exists for novella and novel; this extends it down to flash and short story.
- Offline: yes. Size: S (target and bar), M (cut list).
- Evidence: P1, P4, P10 (editors state hard limits).

### I5. "Is this the right size?" questions (craft prompt, not a verdict)
- What: A short, skippable set of questions on the story spark screen: how many people does the story need? how much time does it cover? can the central want be answered in one scene sequence, or does it persist? Show where the answers point (flash, short, novella, novel) with the reasoning visible and an explicit "ignore this". Draws on Halliday, AutoCrit and DeFreitas ("Too Much").
- Fit: Q1, Q2, Q4 (teaches by use), Q5. Matches belief 5 ("short stories, novellas and novels are the same craft at different lengths") and avoids mandating structure. Must not produce a verdict presented as fact; confidence in the heuristics is MED.
- Offline: yes (static content). Size: S.

### I6. Endings and openings checks (questions, no AI)
- What: At the end of a draft, an optional checklist drawn from the sources: Does the last line rest on an image or an action rather than a stated meaning? Does it echo an earlier object or phrase? Does it keep a promise made earlier? Does the opening make clear who, where and what the wall is within the first page? Wording in the voice of the existing prompts; "suggestions only".
- Fit: Q1, Q2, Q4, Q5; consistent with the "craft we teach" section (know the last line before the first). Overlaps with other researchers' areas (craft); keep it brief.
- Offline: yes. Size: S.
- Warning: do not auto-detect or rewrite. No "fix my ending" button.

### I7. Cover-letter note, not a generator
- What: A small note with the editor consensus (keep it short; do not summarise; at most three credits; "Thank you for considering my story" is enough) and a plain text box saved with the submission entry. No templates that write the bio for the writer.
- Fit: Q2, Q3. Size: S. Low value; include only if cheap.
- Reject: any "AI cover letter" feature (ghostwriting adjacent and magazines are banning AI-assisted work).

### I8. Submission-ready snapshot ("Version sent")
- What: When the writer logs a submission, offer to take a snapshot labelled with the market and date, using the existing snapshot system. Later, if a magazine asks for a revise-and-resubmit, the writer can diff or restore.
- Fit: Q2, Q6; builds on shipped snapshots. Size: S once I2 exists.

### I9. Optional "tries" counter that celebrates sending, off by default
- What: A quiet line like "You have sent 12 stories this year" (writer-owned numbers only). Some writers use "100 rejections a year" as motivation (Submitit; the 100 Rejections Club exists as a community: https://100rejectionsclub.substack.com/p/0033-you-can-go-your-own-way-to-track). Offer it as opt-in, never as a goal or a streak.
- Fit: borderline on Q3 (it is true and non-shaming if opt-in, but turns rejection into a target that can cause its own pressure). Evidence that it helps is anecdotal (LOW-MED).
- Verdict: allow only as an opt-in count of what the writer sent. Reject streaks, "lost-streak" messages, or any notification.

### I10. Contest and prompt support (small)
- What: Allow a story to be tagged "for contest" with a deadline and word limit that show on the home screen only if the writer sets them. A built-in prompt set for flash (constraint-based: a 100-word drabble, a story in 6 words, a story as a recipe or menu, after Jennings' physical-constraint idea) as suggestions.
- Fit: Q1, Q4; fits the existing unblock prompts. Offline yes. Size: S.
- Evidence: only a general craft source (Jennings; Wikipedia on flash forms https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_fiction). I found no evidence on timed contests (the NYC Midnight page returned 404). Low confidence about demand.

### I11. Serial-friendly export and "publish later" kit
- What: Export each chapter as clean Markdown/HTML ready to paste into Substack, Wattpad or a blog, plus a "your whole serial in one file" download, so the writer is never locked into a platform that might close (Kindle Vella).
- Fit: Q2, Q6, belief 6 (the writer owns the work). Exports already exist (Markdown, EPUB); this is mostly copy and one clean HTML export. Size: S.
- Evidence: P13 (platform closure documented; writer pain first-hand not gathered).

### I12. Market database inside the app
- Idea: bundle a catalogue of magazines with word limits, fees and pay.
- Verdict: do not build now. Stale data would mislead, the data belongs to the Grinder, Duotrope and Chill Subs, and maintaining it is a large ongoing job. Possible modest alternative: let the writer paste a market's guidelines or limits into the submission entry (word limit, window dates, simultaneous allowed), and optionally link out. Size: S for the manual version; L for a maintained database (not recommended).

### Rejected or flagged ideas
- Reject: any feature that writes or rewrites an ending, an opening, a query or a cover letter.
- Reject: "AI matches your story to markets" using the writer's text, since it needs uploading work (violates no harvesting, offline and ownership).
- Reject: submission "streaks", "rejection quota" nags, or push alerts about response times (manufactured urgency).
- Reject: uploading a writer's submission history to build community stats like Duotrope or Chill Subs do. If community stats ever matter, it must be explicit opt-in, anonymised, and not default. Flag for the coordinator as a philosophy conflict.
- Reject: showing acceptance-rate statistics as marketing ("only 1 percent get in!") to motivate. Overclaiming and anxiety.

### Suggested order
1. I1 manuscript export. 2. I2 submission log with CSV and I8 snapshots. 3. I4 targets. 4. I5/I6 as small craft questions. 5. I3 waiting view. 6. I11 serial export. Others later or not at all.

## 5. Open questions and gaps

1. Exact Shunn rules. I could read only the opening of Shunn's guide. Before building I1, someone should read all pages of https://www.shunn.net/format/story/ (scene break marker, italics versus underline, end marker, byline versus real name, handling of dialogue and ellipses) and decide whether to include "Shunn Classic" (Courier). Also check .docx header behaviour in Word and Google Docs.
2. How many writers use which tracker? No survey was found. All claims that "most use spreadsheets" are anecdotal (two newsletter posts). A quick poll is the only way to know.
3. Do writers want the tracker inside their writing app, or separate? Evidence of pain is strong; evidence that they want it in the same place is inferred only from the fragmentation complaint.
4. Serial fiction: I did not get first-hand accounts of the real struggles (cadence, burnout, audience, monetisation, loss of platform). Needs a dedicated pass (Royal Road, Substack, Wattpad forums, Reddit r/litrpg and r/royalroad, Kindle Vella author posts).
5. Simultaneous-submission norms and how writers manage withdrawals. Not researched beyond one magazine's policy and ARDOR's advice. Needs data on how common "no sim subs" is (Duotrope and the Grinder know; I could not fetch their stats pages, and Duotrope returned 403).
6. Contests and prompts: no solid evidence gathered (NYC Midnight link failed). Open question whether timed contests deserve any support.
7. Reading as a writer and short stories as practice for novels: not researched due to the search budget; only Kyell Gold and Devon Halliday were found. Ask researchers on craft and learning to cover.
8. Evidence is skewed to speculative-fiction and literary-magazine culture (Clarkesworld, Grinder, Diabolical Plots). Genre romance, mystery, children's and non-English markets, and anthology calls are under-covered.
9. Rejection statistics are mostly second-hand. The "rejected about 20 times" claim is repeated from a 2015 Atlantic piece I did not read. Do not use these numbers in the app's copy (philosophy: do not invent statistics).
10. Whether to ever opt-in share anonymous response times. A philosophy decision, not a research one. Default answer from PHILOSOPHY.md: no.
11. Wattpad and Kindle Vella specifics came from Wikipedia summaries only.
